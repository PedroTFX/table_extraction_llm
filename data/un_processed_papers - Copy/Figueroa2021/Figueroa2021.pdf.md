# Functional traits linked to pathogen prevalence in wild bee communities

Laura L. Figueroa<sup>1,2\*</sup>, Sally Compton<sup>1</sup>, Heather Grab<sup>1</sup> & Scott H. McArt<sup>1</sup>

Reports of pollinator declines have prompted eforts to understand contributing factors and protect vulnerable species. While pathogens can be widespread in bee communities, less is known about factors shaping pathogen prevalence among species. Functional traits are often used to predict susceptibility to stressors, including pathogens, in other species-rich communities. Here, we evaluated the relationship between bee functional traits (body size, phenology, nesting location, sociality, and foraging choice) and prevalence of trypanosomes, neogregarines, and the microsporidian Nosema ceranae in wild bee communities. For the most abundant bee species in our system, Bombus impatiens, we also evaluated the relationship between intra-specifc size variation and pathogen prevalence. A trait-based model ft the neogregarine prevalence data better than a taxa-based model, while the taxonomic model provided a better model ft for N. ceranae prevalence, and there was no marked diference between the models for trypanosome prevalence. We found that Augochlorella aurata was more likely to harbor trypanosomes than many other bee taxa. Similarly, we found that bigger bees and those with peak activity later in the season were less likely to harbor trypanosomes, though the efect of size was largely driven by A. aurata. We found no clear intra-specifc size patterns for pathogen prevalence in B. impatiens. These results indicate that functional traits are not always better than taxonomic afnity in predicting pathogen prevalence, but can help to explain prevalence depending on the pathogen in species-rich bee communities.

Te use of functional traits has been proposed as a way of increasing synthesis, generalizability, and predictability in ecology<sup>1</sup>, all of which are critical elements in a world characterized by accelerating global change and biodiversity loss. Functional traits are measurable properties of an organism, including morphological, physiological, and phenological characteristics, that infuence performance (growth, reproduction, and survival)<sup>2,3</sup>. Functional traits can be efective at predicting plant community assembly, functioning, and structure<sup>1</sup>. Moreover, functional traits can predict how ecologically important taxa, such as bees, will respond to stressors<sup>4,5</sup> and how efective their ecosystem service provisioning may be<sup>6,7</sup>, though not always consistently<sup>8</sup>. Similarly, intraspecifc trait variation can afect pathogen transmission rates by infuencing host susceptibility, immune response, and social behavior<sup>9</sup>; for example, rodents with higher body mass contribute more to tick-born encephalitis transmis sion than rodents with lower body mass<sup>10</sup>. Given the recognized role of pathogens in bee declines worldwide<sup>11</sup> developing generalizable ways of predicting disease risk could help target efective conservation research.

Bee epidemiological research has historically focused on commercial bee species, especially honey bees and bumble bees, and only recently has begun to consider other bee species. Increased molecular screening eforts have found widespread pathogen prevalence in pollinator communities<sup>12–17</sup>, highlighting the need to understand disease dynamics at the community level. Functional traits mediate how individual bees interact with their environment, potentially infuencing exposure to pathogens and likelihood of transmitting the pathogen to other bees, but to date have never been evaluated in relation to pathogen prevalence in bee communities.

Numerous bee functional traits, such as body size, phenology, nesting location, sociality, and diet could infuence pathogen prevalence. Body size is correlated with foraging distance in bees18, and could therefore influence access to foral resources across larger spatial scales. Furthermore, bee body size not only varies substantially within species but can infuence interactions with specifc foral morphologies and subsequent probability of encounter with pathogens on fowers<sup>19</sup>. For example, the common eastern bumble bee, Bombus impatiens, can vary greatly both in size and in likelihood of harboring pathogens, even within the worker caste. Size can be a predictor of bee disease dynamics in this species, with bigger workers developing lower infection intensity of the trypanosomatid gut pathogen Crithidia bombi, independent of the dose provided<sup>20–22</sup>, possibly driven by a more robust immune system in larger bees<sup>22</sup>. As such, variation in body size among and within species could be linked to likelihood of encounter with pathogens on fowers and subsequently pathogen prevalence in bees at the community level.

Bee species difer in the time of year when they are most active as well as for how long. Tese phenologica traits could infuence pathogen transmission and prevalence. For example, bees eclose uninfected with C. bombi, acquiring the pathogen while foraging on contaminated fowers (social and solitary bees) or from nest mates (social bees)<sup>19</sup>. Bees that eclose when the incidence of disease is highest, are at greater risk of exposure, which could in turn result in greater pathogen prevalence. Whether infections accumulate or are cleared over time is not known for most bee species, nor whether patterns difer for pathogens that are exclusively transmitted horizontally, such as C. bombi, compared to other pathogens, such as Nosema spp. (microsporidia), for which sexual and vertical transmission may also be possible<sup>23,24</sup>. Diferences in routes of transmission and persistence under varying environmental conditions could diferentially drive links between phenology and prevalence of diferent pathogen groups.

Nesting location could also infuence pathogen survival, transmission, and ultimately prevalence among bees with diferent nesting locations. High temperature, low moisture, and ultra-violet radiation can reduce the survival of many bee pathogens<sup>25</sup>. For example, C. bombi survives longer on fowers when shaded than when exposed to the sun<sup>19</sup>. Tese environmental conditions are known to infuence choices by ground-nesting bees, and are also likely to vary between above-ground vs. below-ground nests. Some ground-nesting bees prefer warm soil surface temperatures and ample availability of solar radiation when selecting a place to nest<sup>26,27</sup>, which could reduce pathogen survival. However, some bees prefer moist soils for their nests<sup>26</sup>, or secrete waterinsoluble substances into the walls of their nests to regulate moisture<sup>28</sup>. Moisture simultaneously increases the probability of nesting success for many ground-nesting bees while also increasing the probability for pathogen outbreaks, especially for fungal infections<sup>29</sup>. Tus, the nests of ground-nesting bees may facilitate the persistence and subsequent transmission of pathogens either from mother to ofspring (both for social and solitary bees) or between nest mates (for social bees). While larvae of the bumble bee B. terrestris show no evidence of active C. bombi infections afer inoculation, they can transmit the pathogen to brood-care worker bees, highlighting the multiple routes of pathogen transmission possible in the nest<sup>30</sup>. As such, ground-nesting bees may be more likely to harbor pathogens than above-ground-nesting bees.

Sociality could infuence pathogen transmission and ultimately prevalence in bees, though the directionality is unclear. Group living has numerous costs and benefts related to disease susceptibility and pathogen transmission<sup>31</sup>. For example, social bees have evolved stronger antimicrobial defenses than their solitary counterparts<sup>32</sup> and many have hygienic behaviors that reduce the probability of disease spread in the colony and increase recovery rates from infection<sup>17,33,34</sup>. However, social living does increase contact rates that could facilitate outbreaks within a colony; for example, social feeding (trophallaxis) and higher density of individuals are all factors that could promote disease transmission<sup>5,25</sup>. Furthermore, the efect of sociality on pathogen prevalence may be context dependent and be governed not only by group size but also internal organization<sup>35</sup>, which may vary among social bee species. In controlled experiments, having a higher initial C. bombi prevalence in B. impatiens colonies increased the fnal prevalence, a pattern not found for honey bees and N. ceranae<sup>34</sup>. As such, the expected relationship between sociality and pathogen prevalence in diverse bee communities is unclear.

Diet and bee foraging choices can infuence pathogen transmission and prevalence. Te fower species visited by bees could infuence pathogen dynamics due to diferences in transmission potential<sup>19,36,37</sup> and/or antimicrobial properties of pollen and nectar<sup>38,39</sup>. In particular, sunfower pollen has been shown to markedly reduce C. bombi infections in bumble bees<sup>39</sup>. Tis pattern holds across numerous sunfower cultivars and at least one other species in the same plant family<sup>40</sup>, though patterns may be more widespread in the Asteraceae family (Figueroa, et al. unpublished data). As such, bees foraging on Asteraceae may be less likely to harbor pathogens, especially trypanosomes, than those foraging on non-Asteraceae fowers.

Here, we collected 613 bees representing 50 species collected across 11 wildfower plantings and screened them for three common bee pathogens (trypanosomes, Nosema ceranae, and neogregarines). We evaluated (1) taxonomic diferences in pathogen prevalence, among bee families and genera, (2) temporal patterns in pathogen prevalence, (3) the relationship between bee functional traits—body size, phenology (range and peak activity), nesting location, sociality, and foraging choice—and pathogen prevalence (289 bees and 31 species), (4) intraspecifc size and pathogen prevalence of the dominant species in our system B. impatiens, and lastly (5) the model ft based on taxonomy compared to functional traits. Infection with trypanosomes, such as C. bombi, can alter bumble bee foraging behavior, cognitive function<sup>41,42</sup>, and reproduction<sup>43</sup>. Similarly, the microsporidian N. ceranae and neogregarines such as Apicystis bombi can reduce adult bee survival<sup>44–46</sup>. Additionally, larvae inoculated with N. ceranae can have higher morality than sham-inoculated counterparts (mason bees)<sup>47</sup> and reduced adult survival (honey bees)<sup>48</sup>.

## Material and methods

Field sample collection. York, USA, from June 17 to September 11, 2015 as described in Figueroa et al.<sup>16</sup>. Te 10 m × 15 m plots were established in 2012 with a mix of native perennial wildfower species as well as spontaneous weedy species (in total 23 plant species and 10 plant families; Tables S1, S2). We sampled bees within the wildfower planting and adjacent areas for 1.5 ± 0.5 person-hours from 8:00 to 17:00 h at each site on sunny and low wind days, with 8 to 12 collection days per site throughout the season. Sites were sampled in alternating order so that each site was visited at diferent times of day throughout the season. Bees were collected while foraging on fowers and immediately placed on dry ice in the feld and stored at − 80 °C until processing<sup>16</sup>.

Pathogen screening. We screened for presence of Nosema ceranae, trypanosomes, and neogregarines in the bee guts using established Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) primers<sup>16</sup>. We surface sterilized each bee before extracting the gut, fat body, and Malpighian tubules and placing into sterile vials containing two sterilized 2.4 mm steel beads, 100 µl of sterilized 0.1 mm zirconia beads, and 800 µl of TRIsure reagent (Bioline, Boston, MA, USA). Afer homogenizing the samples for 30 s at 6.5 m/s using a bead mill homogenizer (Omni International, Kennesaw, GA, USA), we transferred the solution to a new sterile vial, taking care to not pipette beads or large tissue fragments. We extracted DNA following the manufacture’s protocol. Each extraction batch included a negative control. Te PCR primers are provided in Table S3. While the primers for trypanosomes and neogregarines were developed to be taxonomically inclusive, upon Sanger sequencing a subset of the samples from numerous bee species we found that 66% of the trypanosome positive samples had very high sequence similarity with C. bombi (others present were C. mellifcae, Herpetomonas pessoai, and Trypanostomatidae sp.) and that 100% of the neogregarine positive samples had very high sequence similarity with A. bombi<sup>16</sup>.

Bee identifcation. To reduce risk of DNA degradation and cross-contamination, we identifed the bee species post-dissection. As such, not all specimens retained the characters necessary for identifcation. Bees were predominantly identifed using reference materials located in the Cornell University Insect Collection and published keys<sup>49–54</sup> (additional information in Figueroa et al. 2020). Bees in the genus Lasioglossum are difcult to identify morphologically and were thus identifed using barcoding methods. Specifcally, we amplifed and sequenced a 900 bp region of the protein-coding gene elongation factor 1-alpha<sup>16</sup>. Ultimately, we were able to identify 86% of all bees to species, 13% to genus only, and unable to identify 1% of the bees. Unidentifed bees were removed from the analyses (n = 8). To be conservative, we conducted analyses only for bees that amplifed positive control (bee DNA) or pathogen DNA (separate because multiplex primers developed to favor pathogen amplifcation).

Functional trait assessment. We selected six functional traits that we hypothesized could be important for determining pathogen prevalence: body size (continuous; empirically determined), phenology (continuous; peak and range), nesting location (above-/below- ground), sociality (social/solitary), and foraging choice (Asteraceae/non-Asteraceae). Tese traits are frequently used in the bee literature<sup>8,55,56</sup> and we were able to obtain information for many of the species in our study (Table S4). We determined body size by measuring the intertegular distance (ITD), defned as the distance between the bees’ wing bases (Cane, 1987; Greenleaf et al., 2007) using an Olympus SZX10 microscope and CellSens Standard sofware (Olympus Corporation of the Americas, Scientifc Solutions Group, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). Due to risk of cross-contamination and DNA-degradation, we measured ITD post-dissection. As such, some specimens were not in suitable condition for adequately measuring ITD (76 individuals). To include the specimens that were not directly measured in community-wide analyses (but not intraspecifc analyses), we used the average ITD of other bees of the same species and sex at the same site (reduced number of individuals without measurement to 35). Bee phenology was obtained from a publicly available database of Northeastern US bees<sup>56</sup>. Specifcally, we evaluated mean Julian day of adult activity as well as phenological range of activity (90th quantile—10th quantile in Julian date of adult activity), hereafer referred to as peak seasonal activity and seasonal activity range.

To avoid using nesting-trait categories that would have been represented by few species, we classifed stemand wood-nesting as “above-ground-nesting”, compared to ground-nesting bees as described in Williams et al. 2010. We also consolidated sociality according to Forrest et al. 2015, in which social bees include communal and facultative social species, as compared to obligate solitary species; we collected a single brood parasite (Coelioxys ruftarsis), and so we did not include brood parasites as a separate category. We evaluated the plant family on which the bee was collected, and characterized it as either Asteraceae or non-Asteraceae, hereafer referred to as foraging choice. We made this selection both because there were low number of representatives for many plant families and because of evidence that many Asteraceae have antimicrobial properties against bee pathogens (Figueroa et al. unpublished data). Te full list of bee species and traits is in Table S4. Honey bees were only collected in the latter half of the sampling period<sup>16</sup>; however, given that there were no temporal trends in pathogen prevalence (see “Results”), we feel confdent that this did not infuence our results.

Statistical analyses. Statistical analyses were conducted in RStudio (R Core group, version 3.4.0) using the lme4 and epiR packages<sup>57,58</sup>. We computed the true pathogen prevalence across all sites for each of the three pathogen groups (trypanosomes, N. ceranae, and neogregarines; n = 613) using the epi.prev function of the epiR package, which accounts for diferences in abundance. Te test sensitivity was set to 95% using the Blaker method for two-sided confdence intervals<sup>58</sup>. Tis method was also used to evaluate pathogen prevalence for the most abundant species, Bombus impatiens (n = 106). We evaluated temporal patterns of pathogen prevalence using generalized linear mixed efects models (GLMM), which included pathogen status (proportion of pathogen positive bees out of the bees screened at each site) as a binomial response, month as the numeric explanatory variable, and site as a random efect. We conducted a likelihood ratio test to determine the signifcance of coef fcients by comparing against a null model that only included the random efect (site). Te unit of replication was the number of unique site-month combinations in which we sampled and screened bees (n = 40). All pathogen prevalence analyses included a binomial distribution and logit-link as transformation.

Before evaluating the relationship with functional traits, we evaluated whether there were taxonomic signals in pathogen prevalence in the bee communities. We constructed GLMMs that included pathogen presence/ absence in individual bees as the binary response (conducted separately for trypanosomes, N. ceranae, and neogregarines), with bee family as the explanatory variable and bee species and site as random efects. Te models included sex as a covariate. To determine signifcance, we conducted a likelihood ratio test that compared the model including family as the predictor to one that only included the random efects (n = 466 bees). We similarly evaluated diferences among bee genera. We used family and genus as the predictors instead of species because some genera and species only included very few representative individuals (sometimes only one; Table S4). When the model was signifcant, a post-hoc test was used to determine signifcant diferences among families or genera (Tukey’s HSD).

We evaluated the relationship between bee pathogen prevalence and bee functional traits using a GLMM that included pathogen presence/absence in individual bees as the binomial response predicted by each of the six traits (size, peak seasonal activity, seasonal activity range, nesting location, sociality, and foraging choice), including species and site as the random efects. Given that female and male bees can vary in size<sup>59</sup> and can both become infected<sup>60–63</sup>, models included sex as a covariate. While we successfully screened 613 identifed bees, we analyzed all traits simultaneously in a single GLMM and hence only used species without missing trait or sex data (n = 289 individuals). We conducted this analysis separately for each pathogen group (trypanosomes, N. ceranae, and neogregarines). Including sociality raised the VIF above $2 ;$ once this factor was removed from the model, the VIF was less than two for all remaining factors and were therefore analyzed simultaneously<sup>64</sup>. Based on the taxonomic analysis, we found that Augochlorella aurata was signifcantly more likely to have trypanosomes than other genera (this species was the only representative of the genus in our system; see “Results”). To deter mine whether patterns were driven by this species, we excluded A. aurata from the datasheet and re-analyzed signifcant models. To further evaluate whether season could be driving size patterns, we evaluated diferences in bee size over the sampling period by constructing a Linear Mixed Model that included bee size (ITD) as the response predicted by month, including species and site as random efects. Bee size was log-transformed to meet assumptions of normality.

We evaluated whether intraspecifc body size predicted presence of each of the pathogens in the dominant species (Bombus impatiens, n = 106 workers). Tis was the only species for which we had sufcient numbers to evaluate intra-specifc trait variation $( i . e . \ge 5 0 $ individuals<sup>1</sup>). To address this question, we conducted a GLMM that included pathogen presence as the binary response, predicted by individual bee size, and site as the random efect; this was done separately for each of the three pathogen groups. Signifcance was determined using likelihood ratio test that compared the model including bee size as a predictor to a null model that only included the random efect (site). Te analysis was conducted only on worker bees, as no queens or drones were collected. Te intra-specifc size variation followed a normal distribution, with the exception of one very small worker (Fig. S1). Excluding the single outlier had a large efect on the results (the only signifcant pattern was for N. ceranae, which changed from $P { = } 0 . 0 4 2$ to $P { = } 0 . 2 \dot { 7 } 5$ when the outlier was removed). Although we have no reason to suspect this data point was incorrectly measured, here we choose not to highlight this result since it is does not appear to be a general pattern (Fig. S2). As such, in the main text we present the results with the single outlier removed from the analyses.

Lastly, we compared the ft of models that included functional traits as predictors compared to taxonomic groupings (genus) using Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). To do this, we compared GLMMs that included pathogen presence as the response (trypanosome, N. ceranae, or neogregarine) predicted by the six functional traits or genus; both models included species and site as random efects and were evaluated on the dataset used in the functional trait analyses (n = 289). Both models included sex as a covariate. Diferences in AIC were used to rank the ft of models to the data, and ∆AIC ≥ 2 were taken to indicate substantial diferences between models<sup>65</sup>.

## Discussion

Taxonomic afnity and functional traits have both been successfully used to predict susceptibility to pathogen prevalence in species-rich communities, though these frameworks have not yet been applied to understand host–pathogen dynamics in naturally occurring pollinator communities. Here we found that pathogen prevalence varied among bee genera for trypanosomes and Nosema ceranae in the bee communities surveyed, though interestingly no diferences were found among bee families for any of the pathogens screened. Tere were no clear temporal patterns in pathogen prevalence across the bee communities. Conversely, we found that bigger bees and those that have a later peak season of activity were less likely to harbor trypanosomes, such as Crithidia bombi. Te size pattern was driven by Augochlorella aurata, a species with disproportionately high trypanosome prevalence. Tis species, however, was not the driver of the phenological pattern, which remained afer this species was excluded from analyses. Other functional traits were not linked to trypanosomes, and nor were any linked to N. ceranae or neogregarine prevalence. For the dominant species in the communities, B. impatiens, there was no clear relationship between intraspecifc size and pathogen prevalence for any of the three pathogen groups screened. Overall, our work highlights that specifc functional traits can explain pathogen prevalence patterns in diverse pollinator communities and pinpoints future areas for research in bee epidemiology.

We found taxonomic signals in pathogen prevalence, fnding that trypanosome and N. ceranae prevalence varied by bee genera (Fig. 1). Bees in the genus Augochlorella, which were all A. aurata in our bee communities, were more likely to harbor trypanosomes than those in Lasioglossum, Bombus, and Apis. Tis fnding supports a previous study that found high prevalence of the trypanosome C. bombi in Augochlorella<sup>17</sup>, suggesting additional experimental work should be conducted to understand the importance of this genus as a host, including assessing health consequences of infection and transmission potential on fowers. Tere were no signifcant diferences in neogregarine prevalence among genera, nor did comparisons between any two genera yield signifcant patterns for N. ceranae prevalence. Similarly, we found no diferences in pathogen prevalence among bee families, indicating that there may be key players at the species level that vary markedly from community level patterns. It is important to note that pathogen prevalence was determined using molecular methods, and as such, we cannot distinguish between innocuous pathogen DNA and active infections, nor whether patterns are due to greater resistance or less exposure to the pathogens. Tis work highlights the need to experimentally assess the impact of these pathogens beyond honey bees and bumble bees<sup>62,63</sup>, and determine which bee species, such as A. aurata, play key roles in pathogen transmission at the community level<sup>16,66</sup>.

Trypanosome prevalence was linked to functional traits in the bee communities. Specifcally, bigger bees and those with a later peak season of activity were less likely to harbor trypanosomes (Fig. 4A,B). Given the disproportionately high trypanosome prevalence in A. aurata, a species that is relatively small and has a peak season of activity earlier in the summer, we wanted to evaluate whether the patterns remained when this species was excluded from the analyses. We found that there was no longer an efect of bee size, indicating that A. aurata was largely responsible for the efect of this trait (Fig. 4C). Conversely, the pattern of bees with a later peak seasonal activity remained (Fig. 4D). Interestingly, this occurred despite no clear temporal patterns of pathogen prevalence over time across sites (Fig. 2), nor signifcant diferences in bee size over time. Tere are ofen fewer bee species later in the season, as can be seen not only in our data but also more generally in the bee species community of the Northeastern United States (Fig. S4), and bee species likely difer in deposition rates on fowers<sup>66</sup>. Pathogen prevalence is likely infuenced by the number of competent hosts with broadly overlapping phenologies, as transmission on fowers depends on short time periods between an infected individual depositing pathogens on fowers and susceptible incomers foraging on the contaminated fowers<sup>19</sup>. If there are fewer competent hosts and/ or fewer interactions with the same fowers later in the season, the probability of transmission may be lessened, subsequently reducing prevalence. Further evaluations into the role of phenology, visitation frequency, and overlapping use of foral resources in bee disease dynamics is clearly warranted<sup>16,17</sup>.

Intra-specifc variability plays a key role in driving species coexistence, ecosystem functioning, and response to global change, which can be comparable to or even greater than inter-species diferences<sup>1,67</sup>. Individual body size was not linked to increased likelihood of harboring any of the three screened pathogens in the dominant bee species in our study, Bombus impatiens. Other studies have found that bigger individuals of this species are less likely to harbor the trypanosomatid pathogen C. bombi, possibly due to a more robust immune system<sup>20–22</sup>. However, many studies evaluating the relationship between bee size and pathogens have been conducted in the laboratory where bees have access to pollen and sucrose ad libitum. In the feld, bees experience numerous interacting stressors (e.g. decreased foral diversity, increased pesticide exposure, and competition with honey bees<sup>68</sup>), ofen being smaller in more simplifed agricultural landscapes<sup>69–72</sup>. As such, we do not know if small individuals that were infected succumbed to the negative health consequence of the pathogens (and therefore were not out foraging for us to collect and screen), while larger individuals could tolerate the infections and thus bias the patterns. Additionally, smaller bumble bees ofen remain in the hive as brood-care workers while larger bees forage<sup>73</sup>, with the potential to further bias the sampling. A recent observational study similarly found no relationship between bee size and pathogen prevalence in wild bumble bee workers<sup>74</sup>. As such, determining whether landscape scale fltering of bees could increase susceptibility to infections is a necessary future research direction, and highlights the importance of experimental manipulations in the feld with co-occurring stressors<sup>75</sup>.

Not all functional traits were associated with diferences in pathogen prevalence in the bee communities, nor were there sex diferences (Fig. 3). Namely, range of activity period, nesting location, foraging choice (whether collected or not on Asteraceae) were not linked to pathogen prevalence in the bees (sociality was highly collinear with the other functional traits and thus not included in the fnal model). Te comparison between the model ft of the functional trait model and the taxonomy (genus) model difered markedly by pathogen group. Interestingly, while two traits were signifcantly linked to trypanosome prevalence, there was no diference in mode ft between the functional traits and the taxonomy models for this pathogen group. Conversely, the functional trait model provided a signifcantly better model ft for neogregarines and a signifcantly worse model ft for N ceranae. In a previous study, trait-based models evaluating trypanosome (C. bombi) transmission to B. impatiens at fowers had lower AIC values than models based on plant taxonomy<sup>37</sup>. Diverse pollinator communities are ofen characterized by many rare species<sup>76</sup>, and thus employing functional traits may provide a better model ft by requiring fewer parameters, though as our study shows, not always. Given the recognized role of pathogens in bee declines worldwide<sup>11</sup> and their widespread prevalence in surveyed plant-pollinator communities<sup>12,13,16</sup>, developing generalizable ways of predicting disease could guide more efective conservation eforts. Te negative relationship between bee size (foraging distance) and peak seasonal activity (lower species richness) indicate tha experimentally testing whether greater number of fowers could reduce the overlapping use of foral resources and subsequently reduce rates of pathogen transmission may be a fruitful future direction. Furthermore, collecting phenological data through long-term monitoring eforts of bee communities is very important as patterns may shif with climate change<sup>77</sup>, which may be shaped by other functional traits of the bees<sup>78</sup>, with implications for access to resources, pathogen transmission, and ultimately pollinator health. Our work illustrates that there are taxonomic and functional trait signals in the prevalence of some pathogen groups but not others in diverse pollinator communities.
