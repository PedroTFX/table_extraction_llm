# Habitat preference and functional traits influence responses of tropical butterflies to varied habitat disturbance

Suman Attiwilli<sup>1,2</sup>  · Tarun Karmakar<sup>2</sup> · Kavita Isvaran<sup>1</sup> · Krushnamegh Kunte<sup>2</sup>

Received: 16 August 2019 / Accepted: 14 July 2021 / Published online: 2 August 2021 © African Association of Insect Scientists 2021

## Abstract

Biodiversity is declining worldwide due to anthropogenic disturbances. There is a vital need to be able to predict the impacts of these disturbances on ecological communities. Species’ attributes such as habitat preference and functional morphological traits may help in uncovering broad patterns in their responses to anthropogenic habitat disturbance. We examined trait-habitat associations in 251 tropical butterfy species by measuring three morphological traits (body mass, wing length and probosci length) and classifying species according to their habitat preference (forest, ecotone, open). We then correlated these measures (morphological traits and habitat preference) to responses of butterfies to two common habitat disturbances, i.e., a road pass ing through forest, and dominance of an invasive plant Lantana camara. Both evolutionary relatedness (i.e., family afliation) and habitat preference explained considerable variation in butterfy morphological traits, suggesting a relationship between evolutionary relatedness, habitat preference and morphology. Both habitat preference and morphological traits infuenced response to habitat disturbance: (a) forest roads were frequented by smaller butterfies (b) Lantana-dominated patches were depauperate in larger butterfy species and species with long probosces that are usually associated with deep-fowered forest plants, and (c) habitat preference was related to disturbance from both road and Lantana, with forest-specialized butterfy species showing a negative response. These results suggest that tropical butterfies show generalized responses to habitat disturbance according to their habitat preference and functional morphological traits.

Keywords Invasive species · Roads · Lantana camara · Micro-habitats · Morphological variation · Allometry

## Introduction

A major challenge in ecology is to understand changes in species abundance through space and time. This has become especially urgent, considering the impacts of anthropogenic disturbances on the biosphere (Vitousek et al. 1997: Dirzo and Raven 2003; Wagner 2020). Recently, trait-based approaches have been advocated to derive general patterns and predict changes in species composition along environmental gradients (McGill et al. 2006; Webb et al. 2010; Cadotte et al. 2015; Wong et al. 2019). Traits can also be useful to quantify responses of ecological communities to anthropogenic disturbances (Mouillot et al. 2013). Species with particular traits might reduce in abundance or flter out due to disturbance; thus, trait-based indices could present a more consistent picture of impact than taxonomic indices (Mouillot et al. 2013).

A broad set of morphological, ecological and life-history traits have been used to relate responses of ecological communities to anthropogenic disturbances such as climate change. land use change and fragmentation (Hambäck et al. 2007: Barbaro and Van Halder 2009; Öckinger et al. 2010: Vandewalle et al. 2010; Diamond et al. 2011; Newbold et al. 2013; Jain et al. 2016). In a meta-analysis of bees, traits such as nest location (above vs. below ground) and sociality (solitary vs. social) afected species responses to multiple habitat disturbances (Williams et al. 2010). A review of the response of Neotropical vertebrates to forest fragmentation identifed habitat preference as a key predictor; fragmentation seemed to affect forest-dependent species more negatively than their open-habitat counterparts (Vetter et al. 2011). Larger sized butterfies were more vulnerable to extinction from fragmentation in a study of tropical frugivorous butterfies (Shahabuddin and Ponte 2005).

Invasive plants and linear intrusions such as roads are two widespread anthropogenic disturbances in tropical forests. Invasive plants can afect the behaviour, abundance and richness of the native faunal community by providing a novel resource, altering habitat structure or displacing native plant resources (Vilà et al. 2011; Bezemer et al. 2014; Jain et al. 2016). Roads can impact biodiversity by causing road-related mortality, fragmentation of habitat, and edge-efects (Laurance et al. 2009). In a study of dung beetle assemblages in dry savannah and an adjacent site heavily invaded with the invasive plant Prosopis gladulosa, larger species were excluded from the invaded site, possibly due to impairment of their fight ability and resource searching among the invasive thickets (Steenkamp and Chown 1996). In another study, which examined the impact of roads on mammal abundances, larger species appeared to show stronger declines with road densities than smaller species (Rytwinski and Fahrig 2011). A study of temperate butterfies at a landscape scale found that wing-loading ratios, indicating mobility, predicted the response of butterfies to invasive plants (Gallien et al. 2017). The linkage between species traits and responses of to these two disturbances is not adequately understood in tropical butterfies.

Butterfies are often considered as model organisms for ecological studies of insects, and as fagship species in invertebrate conservation. They exhibit a range of sizes, shapes and manner of fight, making them ideal for trait-based studies of habitat change. Body size, proboscis length and habitat associations (here defned as a preference for ‘forest’, ‘ecotone’ or ‘open’ habitats) are key niche-related traits of butterfies. Body size is a composite trait of organisms that is linked to various life-history and ecological traits such as metabolic rate, home range and abundance (Peters and Wassenberg 1983; García-Barros 2000; Brown et al. 2004; White et al. 2007). Characteristics of the proboscis, such as its structure or length, might determine which food resources butterfies can efectively utilize (Corbet 2000; Krenn et al. 2001; Kunte 2007). Habitat preferences of species refect the micro-habitats where they live, seek mates and/or other resources, and complete their life-cycles (Willmott et al. 2017). Thus, any disturbance that changes the micro-habitat niche availability is likely to affect species differently, according to their requirements.

In this study, we examined whether functional morphological traits and habitat preferences of butterfies can help predict response to forest roads and an invasive plant Lan tana camara (henceforth lantana) — two common habitat disturbances in tropical forests. We frst describe the patterns of variation in functional morphological traits (body size and proboscis length), since there are relatively few studies on comparative functional morphology in butterfies (Marden and Chai 1991; Dudley and Srygley 1994; García-Barros 2000). Baseline information on trait variation can be used to test various eco-evolutionary hypotheses on factors shaping the variation (Polo and Carrascal 1999; Melville and Swain 2000; Goodman 2009). For example, in a study of Nymphalid butterfies in Neotropical forests, forewing shape corresponded with aerodynamic requirements of a gliding fight in the forest understorey (Cespedes et al. 2015). We tested how habitat preference is associated with morphological traits in 251 butterfy species. We used higher-level taxonomic grouping, i.e., family afliation of butterfies, as a crude proxy for phylogenetic non-independence, since a species-level or even a genus-level phylogeny of butterfies used in this study is currently unavailable. For the second objective, we hypothesised that a) Open habitat species would show a strong positive association with road, while forest-specialized species would show a negative association, with ecotone species showing an intermediate response b) Nectar-feeding species with either very short or very long proboscis will avoid lantana-dominated patches. Lantana has a long, slender corolla ca 1 cm deep. Species with a short proboscis may not be able to reach the nectar in lantana fowers, while species with very long proboscises, which often feed on long-fowered forest shrubs, may be competitively disadvantaged on lantana fowers (Kunte 2007). Our work showed that functional traits and habitat preferences of butterfies determine their responses to these two disturbance types, which has implications for habitat management and biodiversity conservation in tropical areas.

## Methods

Morphometric measurements: We measured a single adult male of 251 butterfy species comprising fve families. Greater sampling within species was not possible because of limitations of collection permits and other logistical constraints. However, within species variation is likely to be less than variation across species, hence overall trends in the measured traits may remain representative for this large pool of species. We collected the specimens from two biodiversity hotspots in India, i.e., the Western Ghats (Karnataka and Kerala) and the Eastern Himalaya (Nagaland) during multiple feld visits between the months of April – October (summer and monsoon) in 2012 and 2013. We caught specimens from morning till afternoon with insect nets, and took morphometric measurements at the base camp in the same evening. We measured three traits for this study, viz., body mass and wing length (as indices for body size) and proboscis length. Body mass, i.e., weight of the whole specimen, was measured with a digital weighing-balance (SartoriusAC120S, 0.1 mg resolution). Wing length, i.e., the longest straight-line distance from forewing base to forewing apex, and proboscis length, i.e., straight distance between the base and the tip of the proboscis, were measured using a digital calliper (Mitutoyo Digimatic Caliper CD-6" CSX, 0.01 mm resolution).

Habitat preference: We classifed all species into three categories, i.e., forest, ecotone and open, according to their primary habitat preference (Kunte 2008). These are broad categories, refecting preferred primary vegetation type and canopy openness. Forest species, according to the classifcation used in this paper, generally prefer evergreen, semi-evergreen, dense riparian or moist deciduous forests. Ecotone species might be generally found at the edges of evergreen forests, and in dry deciduous to secondary moist deciduous forests. Open species prefer open scrublands or dry grasslands. We excluded species that could not be classifed into a single category from analysis.

Response to habitat disturbance: We observed responses of butterfies to two habitat disturbances, viz., a road passing through forest and lantana, through surveys carried out in the Biligiri Rangaswamy Temple (BRT) Tiger Reserve in the Western Ghats (Karnataka, India). Study site is a protected forest criss-crossed with unpaved roads, and heavily invaded with alien plants, primarily lantana. Studies that measured lantana occurrence and density in 1997 (Murali and Setty 2001) and 2007—2008 (Sundaram and Hiremath 2012) report that, at the landscape scale, lantana abundance increased enormously, from being present in 41% of the plots in 1997 to 81% of plots by 2008, while the density of lantana stems per plot increased threefold in the same time (Sundaram and Hiremath 2012). We demarcated a study area of 1 km × 0.3 km in the moist deciduous forest patch of BRT. To measure responses to road and lantana, we surveyed butterfy species along 100 m belt transects in two micro-habitats within the study area, i.e., the road verge $\mathrm { ( n { = } 1 0 \ i n \ ^ { \cdots } W e t ^ { \cdots } }$ season, i.e., Sep-Oct 2012, n = 7 in “Dry” season, i.e., Feb-Apr 2013) and the forest interior (n = 7 in Wet season and n = 8 in Dry season 2013). The forest interior transects were laid in areas with different levels of lantana cover. Along these interior transects, we quantifed lantana extent as percentage cover, by visually estimating the percentage cover of lantana in two 5 m × 5 m plots, laid on each side of the transect every 10 m. We took the mean of these 20 readings to characterize the lantana cover for that transect. We classified transects with lantana cover < 25% as “low” lantana (n = 3 in Wet season, and n = 4 in Dry season), and> 25% as “high"lantana (n =4 in Wet season, and n =4 in Dry season). We walked each road and interior transect (100 m each) 3–5 times in each season, at a fxed slow pace of 10 min per walk. We obtained species abundance data by noting the identity of every butterfly encountered along the transects. To minimize identification errors and maximise detectability, we noted small understorey butterflies.

i.e., species with a wing length < 3 cm, and active near the ground to mid-canopy, in a 3 m wide belt along the transects. Bigger butterfies, i.e., those with wing length > 3 cm, and active on the ground till canopy level, were surveyed in a 10 m wide belt along the transects. Thus, small canopy-level butterfies, which are difcult to observe, were excluded from surveys. Diferent width transects allowed us to ensure similar detectability for large and small-sized species.

Transects thus capture small-scale behavioural responses of butterflies to the two habitat disturbances, i.e., road through forest and lantana. We used the trait database, mentioned above, to evaluate the associations between traits and response to habitat disturbance.

## Statistical analysis

Relationship between functional traits, taxonomic ranking and habitat preference: We used linear modelling to examine the association between morphological traits (body mass, wing length, proboscis length) and habitat preference, with family identity as a co-explanatory variable. Wing length and proboscis length are expected to increase allometrically with body mass, and initial explorations suggested that the relationship may vary with family identity. Hence, we ran separate linear models for wing length and proboscis length, with body mass, family, and an interaction term between family and body mass as explanatory variables, together with habitat preference. Trait values were $\mathrm { l o g _ { e } }$ -transformed prior to running the models to satisfy assumptions about normality and homoscedasticity of residuals.

Relationship between functional traits, habitat preference and response to habitat disturbance each species was pooled across a type of transect (all road vs. all interior transects; all low vs. all high-lantana transects) after dividing by the number of trials per season per transect in order to standardize for sampling efort. Data were then pooled for both seasons. To quantify response to road and high lantana, we calculated an index of a species aversion to disturbance as: log $( \mathrm { A } _ { 1 } + 1 ) - \log { ( \mathrm { A } _ { 2 } + 1 ) }$ , where $\mathbf { A } _ { 1 }$ was its pooled abundance in the “undisturbed” habitat (i.e., forest interior/low-lantana) and $\mathbf { A } _ { 2 }$ was its pooled abundance in the disturbed habitat (i.e., road/high-lantana). Abundances were logged since the range of abundance values in the dataset was orders of magnitude diferent. “1” was added to abundances before logging to circumvent the problem of logging 0-abundance in some cases. Thus, a positive value of the index indicates an aversion to high-lantana or road, while a negative value indicates a preference for highlantana or road micro-habitats. To test associations between traits and response to habitat disturbance, we ran simple linear models, with an index of aversion as the response variable, and each of the traits, i.e., body mass, wing length, proboscis length and habitat preference as individual predictors. In case of road vs. interior, ecotone and ecotone-open species were pooled into a single category, i.e., ecotone-open, due to a smaller number of species in each individual category, thus getting a factor with three levels (ecotone-open: n = 17; forest: n = 17; open: n = 11). In case of aversion to lantana, we pooled ecotone and open species into a single category, i.e., ecotoneopen, since there were relatively few representatives of each individual category, thus getting a factor with two levels (ecotone-open: n = 14; forest: n = 16). We did not run multiple regressions because (a) we wanted to thoroughly explore individual trait-disturbance relationships, and (b) traits were somewhat correlated and hence could not be included in a single model.

We used R statistical software (R Core Team 2016) for all analysis. An alpha level of 0.05 was used for all statistical tests.

## Discussion

Relationship between functional traits, family and habitat preference: Functional traits of organisms may help in understanding many aspects of their ecology, such as ecosystem function, geographic distribution, community assembly and behaviour (Srygley and Dudley 1993; Webb et al. 2010; Hoiss et al. 2012). We observed considerable variation in functional traits of butterfies, which can potentially shed light on their ecology and evolution.

Lycaenidae and Riodinidae) were supported as mutually monophyletic clades (Heikkilä et al. 2012). These phylogenetic relationships, together with the associations we found between family and morphology, imply strong family-level phylogenetic constraints in morphology. Substantial trait variation remained within families, which might be an outcome of interesting adaptive divergences or other evolutionary processes and need to be further examined.

Habitat preference can lead to adaptive divergence and speciation (Berner and Thibert-Plante 2015). Selective forces in a habitat, such as food availability, micro-climate and availability of refuge sites, can lead to adaptive morphological divergence (Goodman 2009; Da Silva et al. 2014). Frog assemblages using similar microhabitats converged in morphology and performance across clades and continents (Moen et al. 2013). In Nymphalid butterfies, species exhibiting polarized wing refectance were more likely to occupy forest than open habitat, possibly due to the communicative significance of reflectance in the complex light environment of the forest (Douglas et al. 2007). Similar correspondence between phenotype and micro-habitat was found in orbweaving spiders (Blackledge and Gillespie 2004), “purse case” caterpillars of moth genus Hyposmocoma (Kawahara and Rubinof 2013) and fight morphology of butterfies associated with diferent micro-environments in the canopy and understorey (Mena et al. 2020). We found clear traithabitat relations in the pooled dataset of butterflies. Open habitat species were smaller than ecotone and forest species in terms of both body mass and wing length. The results are broadly consistent with previous studies of tropical butterfy assemblages, where larger and darker butterfies (which heat up faster) were associated with cooler habitats such as closed-canopy rainforest or higher elevations, while smaller and lighter butterfies were more common in hotter, open woodlands or lower elevations (Xing et al. 2016, 2018). In our study of habitat preference, ‘forest’ generally refers to hilly or mountainous forested habitats, whereas ‘open’ refers to open canopy dry grasslands, shrublands or other plains areas which tend to be sunnier and hotter. Our fndings support Bergmann’s rule, which proposes a general increase in body size in colder climates due to better conservation of heat by larger body sizes.

We couldn’t detect any signifcant diference in proboscis lengths of butterfies across habitats, suggesting other selective forces not correlated to our habitat categories at play. Butterfies are adapted to feed on a variety of food sources besides foral nectar, such as pollen, bird droppings, rotting fruit, tree sap, wet mud and carrion (Kunte 2000; Krenn 2010). The form and function of mouth parts and feeding behaviour of adult butterfies may show adaptive variation with respect to their feeding guilds or specialized feeding habits (Krenn et al. 2001; Molleman et al. 2005; Krenn 2010; Bauder et al. 2013, 2015a). These evolutionary mechanisms require further examination in order to explain the variation in proboscis length found in our study, which is the frst to quantify such variation in species found in these biogeographical regions.

Relationship between functional traits, habitat preference and response to habitat disturbance: We found butterfy traits to be associated with habitat disturbance. Forest species were more common in undisturbed areas, i.e., the forest interior and low lantana transects, whereas ecotone and open species were more common in disturbed areas. Body mass and proboscis length also showed a positive association with less disturbed areas.

Road provided an open-canopy micro-habitat with a grass and herb understorey (A.S & K. L., unpublished). Lantana was strongly negatively correlated to canopy cover in the forest interior transects (A.S & K. I., unpublished). This could have led to immigration, or an increase/local shift in abundance of sun-loving open habitat butterfies to the road and high lantana areas in the forest interior, whereas shadeloving forest species were more restricted to low lantana transects in the interior. The results support earlier studies of tropical butterfies where habitat degradation (such as road building, selective logging) and warmer temperatures negatively afected shade loving and forest-associated species (Hamer et al. 2003: Bonebrake et al. 2016). Thermoregulation and the relationship between larger body size and heat gain (Xing et al. 2016, 2018) may also partially explain the increased aversion to road and high lantana (both associated with a more open and hence hotter micro-habitat) apparently shown by larger butterfies in our study. The weaker response of body size to road could possibly be due to usage of road for diferent purposes such as local movement or dispersal by butterflies of different sizes as well as presence of both small (e.g. Mycalesis junonia) and large butterfies (e.g. Melanitis leda) in the forest interior, perhaps refecting the heterogeneity of micro-habitats associated with lantana and non-lantana areas. These micro-habitats are likely to be associated with other climatic factors like humidity, larva and adult food resources, vegetation structure, predation etc., all of which are important factors afecting butterfy distribution in tropical forests (Ghazoul 2002; Dumbrell and Hill 2005; Checa et al. 2014; Nyafwono et al. 2015; Cheng et al. 2018; Beirão et al. 2021). The precise mechanisms behind local distributions of butterflies and responses to habitat disturbance require further examination

Interaction between proboscis length of butterfies (along with other morphological traits such as wing loading) and foral traits such as corolla depth, nectar quality and quantity determines access and preference of butterfies to diferent foral resources (Corbet 2000; Tiple et al. 2009; Mertens et al. 2021). For species with long proboscis, it is more proftable to feed on deeper fowers with greater nectar reward per fower (Kunte 2007; Bauder et al. 2015b). Higher frequency of occurrence of species with longer proboscis length in low lantana transects could be due to a preference for patches with deep-fowered forest shrubs or trees (e.g., Gangara and Notocrypta feeding from fowers of Costus and zinzib ers), which are more likely to occur in these micro-habitats. Alternatively, the association between long proboscis and low lantana micro-habitat could be an artefact of a positive correlation of proboscis length with body size, which was found to be negatively associated with lantana. Behavioural foraging observations of butterfy species on diferent foral resources within the habitat and measurements of foral traits and nectar availability would help to disentangle efects of proboscis length and foraging from other factors afecting butterfy responses to non-native plant resources like lantana.

Note that many butterfy species were rare in the datasets and signifcant unaccounted variation remained in the linear models. Targeted sampling of rare species in future studies should help bring out the patterns of association between traits and habitat disturbance more strongly. This should be complimented with behavioural observations of species and measurements of ecological variables in order to understand the mechanisms behind distribution patterns and their association with traits.

Forest roads and invasive plants can be viewed as causing internal habitat fragmentation, which can potentially change habitat structure, micro-climate, and quality, quantity or confguration of resources. This study provides evidence that even highly mobile taxa such as butterfies may respond predictably to habitat disturbance even at small spatial scales, and that species traits may be useful in deriving general patterns. The results for both road and lantana indicate that these disturbances can potentially change the community structure of butterfies by favouring smaller, ecotone or open habitat species, while popu lations of larger or forest species might be negatively impacted. Moreover, these traits correspond with broad eco-evolutionary diferentiation between species such as body size in association with habitat preference. Traits associated with larger scale ecological patterns such as distribution across habitats can potentially explain responses to habitat disturbances at smaller spatial scales. Our work contributes towards understanding how functional traits can be used to predict shifts in community structure in response to habitat disturbance.
