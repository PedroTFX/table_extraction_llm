# Bee functional groups respond to vegetation cover and landscape diversity in a Brazilian metropolis

Letícia Vanessa Graf  · Israel Schneiberg  · Rodrigo Barbosa Gonçalves

Received: 3 April 2021 / Accepted: 28 February 2022 / Published online: 12 March 2022

© The Author(s), under exclusive licence to Springer Nature B.V. 2022

## Abstract

Context Urbanization is one of the largest causes of habitat loss and fragmentation, factors that modify community structure and can lead to species extinction. Since bees are the main pollinating agents in most ecosystems, losses of bee diversity can have negative consequences on plant diversity and associated services.

Objectives This study aims to understand how bees and their functional traits, such as social behavior, nesting sites, trophic specialization and kleptoparasitism, are afected by an urban landscape.

Methods Ten sites were selected with different levels of urbanization. Bees were sampled with entomological net for 6 h, one day per month, for 10 months.

The response variables evaluated were species diversity, number of bees, and the richness of bee functional traits. Nine landscape metrics were used as predictors, including the proportion of vegetation cover and landscape diversity.

Results Urbanization predictors negatively infuenced bee richness but not diversity. Specialist bees were the most susceptible to urbanization, followed by ground nesters and solitary bees. Kleptoparasites also responded negatively to diferent urbanization metrics. Above-ground nesters and eusocial bees were more resilient to the urban environment and were not afected by urbanization. The proportion of vegetation cover and landscape diversity were the most important predictors for the preservation of bee diversity, followed by the proximity of fragments and the proportion of grasslands.

Conclusions The most responsive and vulnerable functional groups—particularly specialists—should have conservation priority in cities. Analyses of richness and species diversity should not be decoupled from functional traits since doing so may overlook bees’ complex response to urbanization.

## Introduction

It is estimated that, by the end of the century, the worlds’ human population will have increased from 7.6 to 11.2 billion (United Nations 2017). Approximately 54% of people live in cities, which is set to rise to 66% by 2050 (UNRIC 2018). Among the primary ecological consequences of urbanization are habitat loss and fragmentation, processes that can modify the structure of biological communities and, therefore, eventually lead to species extinction (Fahrig 2003; Mckinney 2006; Jones and Leather 2012; Seto et al. 2012; Vanbergen et  al. 2013) and the disruption of ecosystem services (Tylianakis et al. 2007). Because of the increasing rate of urbanization, there is an urgent need for ecological studies documenting the impact on biological communities as well as the species and functions that are being lost in these environments (Dearborn and Kark 2010). On the other hand, cities that maintain ecological processes can serve as refuges for bees, sustaining pollination services for the urban area and its surroundings (Hall et al. 2017; Banaszak-Cibicka and Żmihorski 2020).

Urbanization results in population losses among the most important groups of insect pollinators (Coleoptera, Diptera, Hymenoptera, and Lepidoptera), which can lead to the local and regional disappearance of numerous species (Jones and Leather 2012; Deguines et al. 2016). Pollinators are essential for maintaining biodiversity since they are responsible for pollinating approximately 90% of angiosperms (Aizen and Feinsinger 2003; Ollerton et  al. 2011). With more than 20,500 described species (Ascher and Pickering 2020), bees refect these changes to a greater degree than other groups because they must feed on foral resources such as pollen and nectar (Ollerton et  al. 2011; Cardinal and Danforth 2013). Their ecological and economic importance is estimated at USD 127 to USD 152 billion per year (Bauer and Sue Wing 2016).

In addition to decreasing species richness, urbanization can increase the density of some well-adapted species, causing biotic homogenization (McKinney 2006; Schneiberg et  al. 2020). Habitat loss and fragmentation of natural areas afect the proportion of available resources as well as the ability of some species to move through the landscape, and as such are detrimental to bee survival (Fahrig 2003; Niebuhr et al. 2015: Geslin et al. 2016: Boscolo et al. 2017).

Bees have to move through the urban landscape between green areas in order to access the resources they need, which can be more difcult for some species than others (Boscolo et al. 2017). In addition to the availability of natural vegetation, environmental heterogeneity has also been shown to be important for maintaining bee populations (Aguirre-Gutiérrez et al. 2015; Boscolo et al. 2017; Nery et al. 2018).

The life-history traits of bee species infuence the way they exploit the landscape and resources around them (Boscolo et al. 2016). For bees, functional traits like social behavior, nesting sites, trophic specialization, and kleptoparasitism have been shown to infuence their responses to anthropic changes in the landscape (Banaszak-Cibicka and Żmihorski 2012; Geslin et al. 2016; Silva et al. 2017; Cardoso and Gonçalves 2018; Harrison et  al. 2018; Burdine and Mccluney 2019; Wenzel et  al. 2020; Banaszak-Cibicka and Dylewski 2021). This may result in diferences across species in their susceptibility to the impacts of urbanization. For example, Cardoso and Gonçalves (2018) reported a drop of approximately 50% in the richness of solitary bees in an urban area between 1981 and 2015, while the highly eusocial species increased over the same period. Nesting site preference may also afect bees’ response to habitat modifcation. Most bees that nest in the ground appear to be more vulnerable to urbanization than those that nest in wooden cavities or in exposed nests (Geslin et al. 2016).

Some bees depend on a narrow range of host plants, while others supplement their diet with vegetable oils and are considered to be a specialized functional group. Species with high degrees of trophic specialization have shown high sensitivity to anthropic disturbance (Stefan-Dewenter 2003; Winfree et  al. 2011; Threlfall et  al. 2015; Rocha-Filho et al. 2018; Wenzel et al. 2020). Finally, kleptoparasite bees may be a vulnerable group in disturbed landscapes (Shefeld et al. 2013). The female kleptoparasite lays its egg in the nest of a different bee species. and its larva matures on the provisions stored in the host species’ nest (Rozen 2001). Their presence is therefore dependent on their hosts’ abundance, meaning that they respond to disturbances at a higher trophic level.

Changes in the taxonomic and functional diversity of bees as a consequence of urbanization should be investigated in diferent biomes and urban contexts in order to identify efective local and global conservation strategies (Buchholz and Egerer 2020). Our study seeks to evaluate the response of wild bees to the urban landscape structure in Curitiba, a city in southern Brazil. We began by identifying variations in the species composition across study sites, following which we modeled nine landscape metrics. We expect that environmental heterogeneity, vegetation cover, and isolation are the most important explanatory variables for bee richness and diversity, which will disproportionately afect solitary, below-ground nesting, and specialized bees. Below-ground nesters are likely to be sensitive to the proportion of grassland cover, while above-ground nesters will depend on tree cover. Kleptoparasites are likely to be among the most afected by changes in landscape variables.

## Material and methods

## Study area

The study was carried out in the city of Curitiba, the adjacent municipality of São José dos Pinhais, both of which are located in the state of Paraná in southern Brazil (Fig. 1A–B). The Curitiba metropolitan region currently comprises 29 municipalities with a population of 3,615,027 inhabitants (IBGE 2020). The city of Curitiba has 1,933,105 inhabitants, with a population density of 4027 people per $\mathrm { k m } ^ { 2 } { \mathrm { . } }$ , while São José dos Pinhais has 329,058 inhabitants and a population density of 279.16 people per $\mathrm { k m } ^ { 2 }$ (IBGE 2020). The climate in the region is subtropical (average summer temperature of $2 2 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ average winter temperature of $1 7 \ { } ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ , average rainfall of 1480 mm; INMET—Instituto Nacional de Meteorologia). The local plant cover is a mix of Araucaria forests and natural open felds within the Atlantic Forest ecoregion.

We selected ten sampling sites (Fig.  1C, Online Resource 1), nine in Curitiba and one in São José dos Pinhais. Three sampling sites had already been studied for temporal responses of bees to urbanization in previous studies: Site 1 (Taura and Laroca 2001), Site 3 (Cardoso and Gonçalves 2018), and Site 10 (Martins et al. 2013). The other seven sites are located in public parks and military areas. No private areas were selected due to the risk of private green space being built over during the study period. We chose the sites using the following criteria: diferent proportions of vegetation around the central collection point (centroid) in a radius of 2  km; presence of herbaceousshrub vegetation with fowering plants; a minimum distance of 1  km between fragment centroids to ensure sampling independence between sites (Ferreira et  al. 2015; Geslin et  al. 2016); the ability for collectors to carry out survey activities safely.

## Bee sampling

Sampling was carried out one day per month for ten months between August 2017 and May 2018, for a total of ten days of sampling at each site. The sampling period was designed so as to exclude the winter months of June and July when there is minimal bee activity (average temperature of 9.3  °C). Sampling was carried out for six hours per day (9:00 a.m. 03:30 p.m., with a half-hour break) for a total of 60  h per site. No surveys were carried out on cloudy or rainy days due to reduced bee activity. Flower-visiting bees were collected with entomological nets by one collector (LVG). The surveys were carried out within a radius of up to 200 m around the centroid and aimed to visit every fowering plant in the study area during the sampling period. This distance was defned to standardize the sampling area regardless of fragment size, as well as to be a manageable size for a single day of sampling.

We chose to focus on wild bees only, meaning that individuals of the Western honey bee (Apis mellifera) were not collected as including them would jeopardize the sampling and processing of the other specimens. All other specimens collected were prepared for identifcation on entomological pins, dried at temperatures between 40 and 60 °C for three days, labeled, identifed, recorded in a database, and fnally deposited in the Jesus Santiago Moure Entomological Collection (DZUP) at the Federal University of

Paraná. Bee classifcation followed Melo and Gonçalves (2005), where the whole group is treated as a single family (Hymenoptera, Apoidea, Apidae sensu lato). Genera were identifed using Silveira et  al. (2002), following which species-level identifcations were made considering taxonomic revisions (see a summary in the catalog of Moure et  al. 2012) and comparison with DZUP specimens.

Functional groups were identifed using the classifcation of Martins et  al. (2013) and biological information summarized in Michener (2007). The data were reviewed by the expert consultants listed in the acknowledgments section. We used three main categories: behavior, nesting preference, and trophic specialization. For behavior, bees were separated into solitary and eusocial, with the latter group further divided into primitive (totipotent reproductive caste) and highly eusocial (fxed reproductive caste) groups to encompass variation in reproductive behavior. Bees’ were separated according to nesting preferences between those nesting in the soil (ground-nesters) and those that nest above ground (exposed nests, cavities, and other substrates). With respect to trophic specialization, species were separated into specialists (oligolectic and oil bees) and generalists. Due to its particularities, the kleptoparasite group was analyzed separately from the other categories.

## Landscape data

We used a Landsat 8 image of the study area, which has a 30 m resolution. The acquisition date was April 25th, 2016 (ID: LC08\_L1TP\_220078\_20160425\_2 0170326 01 T1; path: 220; row: 78). The georeferencing image underwent geometric correction and atmospheric correction (DOS1) before the classifcation procedure. Image classifcation was performed using the Semi-Automatic Classifcation Plugin (SCP) (Congedo 2016) with QGIS version 3.10.0 (QGIS Development Team 2019). The resulting landuse map was manually post-processed to correct the minor errors of the semi-automatic classifcation. We considered fve units, defned as follows: urban area, water bodies, forest vegetation, shrub vegetation, and grassland vegetation. The grassland unit includes natural open felds, managed grass, exposed soil, and areas covered with ruderal plants. In this study, four bufers were used to obtain the spatial metrics (750  m, 1  km, 1.5  km, and 2  km) (Fig.  1), defned based on the fight area of most bees (Araújo et  al. 2004; Ferreira et al. 2015; Geslin et al. 2016) because fight area and other processes are scale-dependent (Jackson and Fahrig 2015; Miguet et  al. 2016). We also considered a radius of 500 m but did not use it because most study sites were surrounded by a green area that occupied more than 50% of this bufer, impairing the analyses.

We calculated nine landscape metrics based on the five units described above, as follows: Shannon's diversity index of the landscape, proportion of grass cover, proportion of arboreal cover (sum of shrub and forest cover), proportion of vegetation cover (sum of grass, shrub, and forest cover), largest patch index, edge density, number of patches, mean Euclidean nearest neighbor distance (which measures the patch isolation), and mean shape index. The mean shape index is the simplest way to measure the complexity of the class shapes of a landscape (Flores et al. 2019). Shannon’s diversity index of the landscape was used to describe landscape diversity (environmental heterogeneity), considering the number and proportion of landscape classes (Boscolo et al. 2017; Nery et al. 2018). Its value equals minus the sum of the total landscape proportion of each land cover unit multiplied by its natural logarithm. This metric increases with growing numbers of land cover units and/or when the proportional area of these units becomes more equitable. These metrics refect the landscape composition and confguration and can be expected to afect our ecological variables, with specifc expectations related to the diferent trait groups. All metrics were calculated using Fragstats v.4 (McGarigal et al. 2012).

## Data analysis

Bee diversity, bee richness, and richness of each functional group were selected as the response variables of interest to explore how bee diversity changes along variables refecting increasing urbanization. We used the Shannon index as a diversity index and complemented the bee diversity analysis with a dispersion diagram between species richness and Pielou equability index (J’) (Pielou 1966) for each study site to understand the efect of the presence of eusocial bees on the result (Melo 2008) (Online Resource 2). To describe the diferences in species composition across study sites, the value of beta diversity was obtained using the Bray–Curtis dissimilarity index, which considers species abundance (values close to 0 indicate greater similarity in species composition). Beta diversity was calculated using the betapart package (Baselga and Orme 2012), which allows values to be separated into two components: the replacement of species (turnover) and the loss of species (nestedness) across sites (Baselga 2010).

To select the spatial extent at which each landscape metric best predicted diversity, total richness, and functional group, we performed univariate fts for each metric and scale and selected the one with the highest coefcient of determination $( R ^ { 2 } )$ (Boscolo and Metzger 2009; Moreira et al. 2015; Miguet et al. 2016; Schneiberg et al. 2020). The proportion of vegetation cover and the landscape diversity showed high values of $R ^ { 2 }$ for most of the biological variables evaluated, particularly at the smallest bufer analyzed (750  m) (Online Resource 3). Since the correlation between these two variables at this scale was high (0.88), and both were considered important, the selection of models was carried out separately for each one with the remaining predictors.

Before performing the model selection analysis, we checked for multicollinearity between the predictor variables using Pearson correlation. We removed the variables with strong collinearity (i.e., more than 0.7) with the proportion of vegetation cover or landscape diversity (performed separately for each one), which were the variables with the highest $R ^ { 2 }$ values concerning most of the biological variables. In the event that a pair of variables had strong collinearity (for example, between isolation and mean shape index), we chose the one that had the highest $R ^ { 2 }$

All landscape metrics included in the model selection were centered to zero using the standardize function in the vegan package due to the range of absolute values that varied naturally. In order to access the complexity of the relationships between landscape structure, bee species, and functional diversity, we compared the multiple competing hypotheses using model selection analysis and a selection criterion corrected for small sample sizes (Akaike Information Criterion—AICc). The analysis also provides the diference between the AICc value of a given model and the most parsimonious model (ΔAICc), as well as the weight of evidence $( w _ { i } )$ of each model. Models with $\Delta \mathrm { A I C c } < 2$ were considered equally plausible, and no model was considered when the no-efect model showed ΔAICc < 2. Models with a high weight of evidence have a high probability of being plausible explanations for the selected response variable. The model selection analysis was carried out using the AICcmodavg package (Mazerolle 2019). A Poisson distribution was used for count data (richness) and Gaussian distribution for species diversity. All statistical analyses were performed in R version 3.6.2. with R Studio version 1.2.5033 (R Development Core Team 2016) using the vegan package (Oksanen et al. 2013).

When analyzing the diferent proportions of vegetation cover and Shannon’s diversity index of each sampling site (Fig. 1 and Online Resources 9), Site 1 clearly stood out for its lower values compared to all the other sites. For this reason, the selection of models was also performed without this site to explore the relationships between the response variables and the predictors for the data from the remaining sites.

## Results

A total of 8938 specimens were sampled, belonging to 288 species and the fve subfamilies that occur in Brazil. The abundance of each species and the sites where they were collected can be found in Online Resource 4. The overall value of beta diversity across all study sites was 0.78, with a turnover value of 0.76 and nestedness of 0.02. Of the species collected, 11.8% were kleptoparasites (not included in the other functional groups), 68.8% were solitary, and 19.4% were eusocial (5.6% highly eusocial and 13.9% primitively eusocial). Meanwhile, 55.6% nested in the soil and 32.7% nested above ground, while 20.5% were specialists and 67.7% generalists. The species and their functional groups are shown in Online Resource 5. The values of species richness overall and within each functional group, as well as Shannon’s diversity index for each area, are found in Online Resource 6.

The GLM output resulting in plausible models (ΔAICc < 2) is shown in Table  1 alongside the respective vegetation cover and in Table 2 with landscape diversity. Three models described the variation in total bee richness and showed that bee species richness increased with vegetation cover and landscape diversity. The isolation of the fragments was also considered important for total bee richness, but only in addition to vegetation cover (Tables 1, 2, Fig. 2A–B).

This relationship between vegetation cover and landscape diversity indicated the strongest response among specialist species (assessed through the intercept values), followed by the below-ground nesters and solitary bees, with generalist and kleptoparasite bees the least afected (Tables 1, 2, Fig. 2C–N).

The isolation of the fragments alone was only relevant for the generalist and kleptoparasite groups (Fig.  2K, N). The grassland cover proportion, combined with landscape diversity, was important for below-ground nesters and specialists. Contrary to predictions, landscape variables had no efect (ΔAICc > 2 for all models) on bee diversity, aboveground nesters, or either group of eusocial bees (primitively and highly eusocial) (Online Resources 7, Online Resource 8). The results of the GLM performed without Site 1 showed that the relationship of the functional groups that showed the highest vulnerability to urban landscapes with vegetation cover and with landscape diversity is maintained, namely below-ground nesters $( w _ { i } = 0 . 4 0$ and 0.57, respectively; α = 0.09 and 0.08, respectively) and specialist bees (wi = 0.61 and 0.60, respectively; α = 0.18 and 0.17, respectively).

## Discussion

Only about half of the bee species (66) were found in the most urbanized sampling unit (Site 1), which had 7% vegetation cover. This is a considerable loss of species compared to the remaining sites, with 111 to 134 bee species (Online Resource 6). These nine other study sites had a vegetation cover of 33% within a 750  m radius. Our result is consistent with fndings in the literature, where a drop-of in bee richness is observed at a threshold of around 35 to 40% vegetation cover (Ferreira et al. 2015; Boscolo et al. 2017; Wenzel et  al. 2020). Even though there is no data between the 7 and 33%, this consistency across diferent locations indicates that this threshold value may be useful in determining conservation strategies based on the proportion of green areas in urban landscapes.

The overall beta diversity value of turnover among the study sites was high, showing that high species replacement was occurring, meaning that the bee community changed considerably around the city. On the other hand, total bee species loss (nestedness), a richness metric, had very low importance for the overall beta diversity. Such fnding suggests that richness is only one criterion for conservation strategies, as each species and functional group has particular survival needs. This result is congruent with the selection of models performed, demonstrating that the overall efect of urbanization on bee populations is diferent from the efect on specifc functional groups.

Through the selection of models, we found that bee richness, but not diversity, is associated with vegetation cover and landscape diversity in Curitiba, contrary to expectations. Examining diversity, we speculate that bee diversity in our system could be infuenced by the high abundance of a few highly eusocial stingless bees (Online Resource 2). A problem with diversity indices is that they encompass values of richness and equability, which can mask the responses of individual species (Melo 2008; Wilson and Jamieson 2019). Many studies that focus on the magnitude of the efect of landscape structure on species diversity and richness have assumed that all species are functionally equivalent, but bee species have diferent social organizations, nesting, and plant preferences (Michener 2007), and these functional traits lead to diferent responses to urbanization (Grime 1998; Hubbell 2001; Cariveau and Winfree 2015; Martins et al. 2017).

Stingless bees are an important component of the eusocial functional group in tropical areas. Their perennial colonies with stored food can be valuable in disturbed habitats with scarce resources, such as cities (Zanette et al. 2005). Usually, the most abundant species in the Brazilian cities belong to this tribe. Trigona spinipes (Fabricius, 1793) is one of the most resilient species in Curitiba (Cardoso and Gonçalves 2018), as well as in other cities around the country (Zanette et al. 2005; Jafè et al. 2016). We also found a high abundance of the small-sized Plebeia Schwarz and Tetragonisca Moure species. Because the sampled stingless bees are small (< 1  cm) and therefore fy short distances, they may be expected to be more sensitive to urbanized landscapes, responding to smaller-scale changes (Stefan-Dewenter et  al. 2002). However, this was not the case here, indicating that bee size alone may not refect the overall sensitivity. In contrast, there are some large species of Meliponini, like species of Melipona Illiger, which can be vulnerable to urbanization (Ferreira et  al. 2015)—one example is M. bicolor Lepeletier, 1836. These species need large tree hollows to nest (Silva 2008), which are not usually found in urban sites. Their nests are also not found in urban cavities such as walls.

Although eusocial bees comprise less than 20% of all species, they are more abundant. Neither the fxed caste nor the totipotent eusocial subgroups responded to urbanization. These groups include totipotent bumblebees, small carpenter bees, and sweat bees, which have been shown to be resilient, with high abundance even in highly urbanized areas. This indicates that the presence of a colony, even ephemeral, and associated longer foraging periods may be related to lower sensitivity (Michener 2007). While social species occur throughout the urban landscape without signifcantly decreasing their richness (Oliveira et  al. 2009; Aguirre-Gutiérrez et al. 2015; Ferreira et al. 2015; Silva et  al. 2017; Cardoso and Gonçalves 2018), this pattern drastically changes in agricultural sites, where they are much more sensitive to changes than solitary bees. Researchers have found that social species respond more strongly to patch isolation, probably due to correlated diferences in nesting substrate, and responded more to pesticides, refecting colony bioaccumulation and their greater and longer exposure periods to toxic substances (Williams et al. 2010).

Although bees can nest in a myriad of substrates, they can be broadly classifed into species that nest in the soil and those that explore diferent kinds of above-ground surfaces. This distinction is important because most species nesting in the soil need permeable areas, and their higher response to urbanization is commonly related to high amounts of impermeable areas within the cities (Geslin et al. 2016; Pereira et  al. 2020; Wenzel et  al. 2020). Even with permeable areas, urban green areas usually have extensive surfaces with dry and compacted soil, ofering few opportunities for bee nesting (Xie et al. 2013; Rocha-Filho et  al. 2018). On the other hand, above-ground nesters can beneft from urban forest sites and humanmade cavities, though the above-ground functional group did not respond to the landscape predictors in the present study. Harrison et  al. (2018) observed a negative response to urbanization, but other studies showed that cavity-nesting bees responded positively to the urban landscape, possibly because many species can exploit human constructions as a substrate (Zanette et al. 2005; Wenzel et al. 2020). These conficting results may be related to bee responses to different attributes of cities, such as the degree of urbanization, biome, and local species pool (Rocha-Filho et al. 2018; Buchholz and Egerer 2020).

Bees with specialized host plant relations are the most vulnerable to urbanization, according to our results. These bees are more vulnerable to plant population losses (Packer et al. 2005), especially in urbanized areas (Cariveau and Winfree 2015). Plant losses and homogenization can suppress species that provide pollen and oil for specialist bees. Thus, besides needing a specifc food supply, this group has other biological attributes that respond negatively to urbanization. Notably, all the specialist species we sampled are solitary, and 92% build subterranean nests. This functional conjunction explains why this trait is considered the most vulnerable to urbanization, with greater chances of sufering local extinctions (Biesmeijer et al. 2006; Harrison et al. 2018). One example is the oligolectic bees belonging to the tribe Neopasiphaeini (Almeida et al. 2019), which have declined in Curitiba according to the available historical data (Taura and Laroca 2001; Martins et al. 2013; Pereira et al. 2020) and are extinct in the more urbanized sites. The Neopasiphaeini species usually have only one generation per year, with adults foraging only for a few weeks, another trend that can contribute to their vulnerability.

Therefore, as expected, all the results presented here demonstrate that plant specialist bees, ground nesters, and solitary species are the ones that contribute most to the lower total bee species richness in more urbanized areas. We also found that generalist species responded positively to vegetation and landscape diversity. Examining the distributions in Fig. 2, generalist richness (Fig.  2I, J) has a trend similar to overall bee richness, with a strong contribution of Site 1 (Fig. 2A, B). Indeed, the response of generalist species disappeared when this site was removed from the analysis, indicating that this group is less sensitive to the efects of urbanization.

Kleptoparasites had greater rates of dispersion across sites (Fig.  2L–N). These bees were almost absent at the most urbanized site, as previously reported by Taura and Laroca (2001). Cuckoo bees depend on the presence of their host and the resources it provides (Rozen 2001; Michener 2007), meaning that they are directly afected by landscape disturbances. Thus, a linear response to environmental metrics can be attenuated in some circumstances. A pair of long-term studies by Martins et al. (2013) and Cardoso and Gonçalves (2018) monitored Sites 10 and 3, respectively, and did not fnd evidence of declines in kleptoparasites. As discussed by Shefeld et al. (2013), kleptoparasites can be indicative of the status of the total bee assemblage and, as expected, they were more affected by the metrics evaluated than the bee community at large (assumed by the higher intercept values), indicating that monitoring in urban areas should also focus on these bees.

Biotic homogenization encompasses the processes by which a community loses its biological distinctions at any level of organization, whether genetic, taxonomic, or functional. Functional biotic homogenization is related to greater similarity in species’ roles in a community due to the loss of unique traits (Olden and Rooney 2006). Many studies describe the functional biotic homogenization caused by urbanization in terms of the loss of trophic specialist species (Mckinney 2006; Deguines et  al. 2016; Schneiberg et  al. 2020). However, solitary and ground-nesting groups also declined in urbanized sites, providing a new perspective on how biotic homogenization should be investigated for the group.

Among the nine landscape predictors tested, the proportion of vegetation cover and Shannon’s landscape diversity index had the greatest explanatory power. The former represents the natural cover of habitat in relation to the total area of the landscape and is usually the most common explanatory variable for urban species richness across numerous taxa, including bees (Fahrig 2003; Calvillo et  al. 2010). Some studies have focused on the proportion of impervious surfaces in urban areas as it is assumed to negatively correlate with habitat area (e.g., Geslin et  al. 2016). Here, we used the proportion of vegetation cover, further broken down into diferent units (grass, shrub, and forest cover) to analyze different responses. The second predictor is a measure of environmental heterogeneity, an important predictor noted by some authors (Fahrig et  al. 2011; Moreira et  al. 2015). Bees can beneft from heterogeneous landscapes due to increased food and nesting resources (Aguirre-Gutiérrez et al. 2015; Boscolo et al. 2017; Nery et al. 2018), as do other pollinators, particularly butterfies (Orlandin and Carneiro 2021).

When analyzing vegetation cover in diferent classes, we found that only grassland cover was signifcant for specialists and ground-nesters. Grasslands provide complementary resources for bees. such as foral and nest-building materials and nesting substrates (Nery et  al. 2018). Open vegetation formations are the most vulnerable areas to urbanization (Boscolo et al. 2017; Nery et al. 2018), frequently neglected by conservation plans compared to forested sites. Although some bees are dependent on forest vegetation, especially some cavity nesters, we did not fnd a signifcant response for any of the functional traits evaluated to this vegetation class. For example, birds have been shown to increase their richness with more forested areas in urban environments (Schneiberg et  al. 2020). This highlights the diferent needs of distinct organisms (Boscolo et  al. 2016), which can require a variety of conservation approaches. Another important prediction was the proximity of the natural fragments, corroborating the importance of functional connectivity for bee survival (Boscolo et al. 2017).

The proportion of grassland cover was highly correlated with the proportion of vegetation cover, which has been removed from the analysis performed with this metric. However, it showed a low correlation with landscape diversity, which proved to be an important metric for the main vulnerable groups in the urban environment (below-ground nesters and specialist bees). This highlights the importance of performing the analysis with the metrics of landscape diversity and vegetation cover separately. Among the other metrics assessed here, only patch isolation proved to infuence the bee richness of some groups, although as a secondary metric.

A recent review on the importance of multi-scale studies underscored the ability of some factors to impair analysis (Miguet et  al. 2016), especially the decreasing of variance values as the scale increases. We did not fnd a relevant decrease from 750 to 2 km, and the landscape diversity variance increased instead (Online Resource 9). The smaller bufer (750 m) had the greatest degree of explanatory power for our data, suggesting that nearby resources and nesting substrate are important. both in Curitiba and other cities (Ferreira et al. 2015; Geslin et al. 2016). Although the maximum fight capacity of many species is greater than the bufer used, bees forage close to their nest, which reduces the landscape scale they respond to (Araújo et  al. 2004; Wright et  al. 2015; Geslin et  al. 2016).

With regard to the satellite image used in this study, we chose Landsat 8, which has a 30 m resolution, because it captures a good estimation of classlevel metrics (e.g., vegetation cover, urban areas), it has multitemporal image availability and free data acquisition. Although Landsat satellites provide medium-resolution imagery, it has been used in many bee-based studies (Machado et al. 2020; Ferreira et al. 2020; Coutinho et al. 2021), which makes comparison between studies less biased. However, high resolution images can improve the precision of the data and also provide more detailed landscape perspective of patchlevel metrics by highlighting corridors and potential stepping stones. In this way, better resolution images like Sentinel (10  m) could be used for this type of studies in the future to obtain more accurate data.

## Conclusion

We studied how bees respond to an urban landscape in southern Brazil and found that plant specialists, ground-nesters, solitary bees, and kleptoparasites have a stronger response to urbanization than other groups. Banaszak-Cibicka and Dylewski (2021) recently argued that species loss to environmental changes could refect losses of some functional groups, and total species richness may not be a good indicator in pollinator communities. We therefore conclude that special attention is necessary to conserve vulnerable groups rather than relying on the resilience of generalist eusocial species, such as some stingless bees. Among the predictors, vegetation cover proportion and landscape diversity had the greatest explanatory power, while distance among fragments and grassland cover were also relevant.

We suggest creating new green areas in the city and promoting vegetation heterogeneity in the existing ones by improving host plants and soil quality. Each country has diferent native species of fowers that attract bees, and their cultivation in gardens and parks should be promoted for bee conservation. In this context, programs that increase the bee-friendly residential gardens are important, as well as the reduction of lawn mowing (Wastian et al. 2016; Baldock et al. 2019). Taken together, these actions help prevent local and regional extinctions.
