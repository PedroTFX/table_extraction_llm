## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Agricultural pollination Apple orchards Bee diversity Ecosystem services Flower-handling behavior Functional traits Pollination

## A B S T R A C T

A growing body of evidence suggests that wild bees play an important role in agricultural pollination. It is very di<sup>fi</sup>cult, however, to accurately quantify the contribution of wild bees relative to honeybees in most crop systems. We quanti<sup>fi</sup>ed the relative contribution of honeybees and wild bees to the pollination of an economically important, insect-pollinated crop (apple). We use an empirical dataset to identify which of three functional traits (body size, pollen load purity, and <sup>fl</sup>ower handling behavior) contribute signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly to seed set. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that <sup>fl</sup>ower handling behavior and abundance were the only functional traits that signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly predict seed set. When we take into account <sup>fl</sup>ower handling behavior and abundance, wild bees contributed signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly more to seed set than honeybees in the apple orchards we surveyed. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that land managers may bene<sup>fi</sup>t from focusing on supporting communities of wild bees, rather than investing in honeybee hive rental.

## 1. Introduction

Pollination services provided by <sup>fl</sup>ower-visiting insects, especially bees, are critical to global food security (Klein et al., 2007). Moreover, a healthy human diet comprises fruits, vegetables, and oils that rely on bee pollination for their production (e.g. Eilers et al., 2011; Kant et al., 1993). While modern agriculture has traditionally used managed honeybees for pollination, global production of pollinator-dependent crops is now out-pacing world-wide production of honeybee hives (Aizen and Harder, 2009). The threat of “pollinator-de<sup>fi</sup>cits” is further com pounded by recent honeybee declines (vanEngelsdorp and Meixner, 2010), largely due to heavy pathogen loads and exposure to pesticides (Goulson et al., 2015). Providing sustainable pollination services into the future, therefore, requires a diversi<sup>fi</sup>cation of pollination strategies, including leveraging the services provided by native and wild pollinato species.

Wild bees contribute substantially to insect-dependent crop production (Garibaldi et al., 2013; Winfree et al., 2008), particularly when wild bee communities are diverse and abundant (e.g. in strawberry (Connelly et al., 2015), watermelon (Kremen et al., 2002; Winfree et al., 2011, 2007), blueberry (Isaacs and Kirk, 2010; Javorek et al., 2002; Tuell et al., 2009), and apple (Blitzer et al., 2016; Park et al., 2015; Russo et al., 2015)). In a global study of 30 crops, wild bees provided superior pollination services (i.e. fruit set) compared to honeybees, regardless of the number of honeybees present (Garibaldi et al., 2013). Divergence in functional traits between honeybees and wild bees may explain this discrepancy in provisioned pollination service. Whereas the honeybee is a single species, wild bees comprise a diverse assemblage of species with an array of functional traits that may in<sup>fl</sup>uence pollination services. For example, the way that bees carry pollen, their host speci<sup>fi</sup>city, their body size, and how they handle <sup>fl</sup>owers may all play a role in pollen deposition and ultimately fruit set production and quality (Thomson and Goodell, 2001; Larsson, 2005; Martins et al., 2015; Park et al., 2015). While a positive relationship between wild bee visitation and crop pollination service is now well-described, less is known about speci<sup>fi</sup>c functional traits of wild bee communities that a<sup>f</sup>ord higher quality pollination.

The relative contribution of wild bees to pollination can be estimated as the summed product of per-visit e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of all species and their relative abundance (Olsen, 1996). Using this measure, Park et al. (2015) found two groups of wild pollinators to contribute less to apple pollination than honeybees due to their lower abundances. These bee groups, however, represented a small portion of the entire wild bee community. Directly comparing the pollination services of honeybees to those of the entire wild bee community is a di<sup>fi</sup>cult task in crop systems, like apple, which have diverse bee assemblages. Wild bees comprise many species, making it logistically di<sup>fi</sup>cult to experimentally measure the per-visit e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness (e.g. pollen deposition, fruit set) of every species.

Our study investigates functional traits that determine the quality of pollination services provided by wild bees for apple, Malus domestica. We <sup>fi</sup>rst identify bee functional traits that predict seed set in apple orchards. We then use these functional traits as a proxy for per-visit e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness to estimate wild bee pollination services relative to that of honeybees at a regional scale within NY. Thus, our measure of pollinator contribution is the summed product of functional traits that predict seed set for each bee species multiplied by their relative abundance. Apple is a high-value, pollinator-dependent crop (Free, 1964; Garratt et al., 2014a) and an ideal system in which to explore the role of functional traits in crop pollination. Wild bees visiting apple blossoms are abundant and diverse (Gardner and Ascher, 2006; She<sup>fi</sup>eld et al., 2013; Russo et al., 2015; Kammerer et al., 2015), and apple fruit and seed set increase directly with wild bee abundance but not with honeybee abundance; (Martins et al., 2015; Mallinger and Gratton, 2015; Joshi et al., 2015; Blitzer et al., 2016). Indeed functional diversity is a strong predictor of pollination service (i.e. fruit and seed set) in apple orchards (Martins et al., 2015; Mallinger and Gratton, 2015; Blitzer et al., 2016). While these studies document bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of functional diversity for apple pollination, they included a variety of traits, including some that were not likely to have a direct e<sup>f</sup>ect on pollination, such as sociality. Our study identi<sup>fi</sup>es speci<sup>fi</sup>c functional traits that may directly a<sup>f</sup>ect pollinator e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness and have not been accounted for together in previous studies: <sup>fl</sup>ower handling, body size, and the composition of pollen carried by di<sup>f</sup>erent bee species.

In this manuscript, we combine data from several studies. We identify functional traits that predict seed set, a direct correlate to fruit quality and value (Garratt et al., 2014b), and then quantify the relative pollinator contribution of bee species detected in NY apple orchards. We compare contributions of wild bees relative to managed honeybees based on the functional traits we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as signi<sup>fi</sup>cant predictors of seed set. Our results indicate that wild bees, especially ground-nesting bees, provide the bulk of pollination services in the majority of sur veyed orchards. Thus, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that wild bees are not only a good insurance policy against honeybee declines, but also a major contributor to commercial NY apple production − both because of their abundance in NY apple orchards and their high propensity to make direct contact with a <sup>fl</sup>ower’s anthers and stigma. Moreover, our results show that wild bees contribute signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly more to seed set relative to honeybees on average across the apple orchards we surveyed.

## 2. Methods

To quantitatively compare pollination services of wild bees to that of honeybees in NY apple orchards, we calculated the summed contribution to seed set of the entire wild bee community relative to that of the honeybee. There are two fundamental components to estimating the contribution of a pollinator or group of pollinators to seed set: (1) abundance (visitation rate) and (2) per-visit efectiveness (Ne'eman et al., 2010; Olsen, 1996). Abundance is relatively easy to measure even when the pollinator fauna is as diverse as that in apples. Measuring per-visit e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of each species, however, poses signi<sup>fi</sup>cant challenges when pollinator diversity is high. Per-visit pollinator e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness is often quanti<sup>fi</sup>ed as the number of pollen grains deposited on a previously unvisited <sup>fl</sup>ower in a single visit (Ne'eman et al., 2010Park et al., 2015). This can be obtained by attaching a <sup>fl</sup>ower to a stick and “interviewing” free-foraging bees. Measures of per-visit pollen deposition have been obtained for a small fraction of the bee diversity in apple orchards. Thomson and Goodell (2001) compared bumblebees (Bombus) to honeybees using this method, and Park et al. (2015) compared bumblebees, honeybees, and members of the Andrena subgenus Melandrena. The interview method can only yield su<sup>fi</sup>cient sample sizes for common species slow enough to place interview

<sup>fl</sup>owers in their foraging path.

We <sup>fi</sup>rst identi<sup>fi</sup>ed functional traits of bees that directly in<sup>fl</sup>uence per-visit e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness and could be applied to all species detected to date in New York apple orchards. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we used model selection to identify functional traits that signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly predict seed set. These signi<sup>fi</sup>cant functional traits were then used to quantify the summed pollination contribution of wild bees and honeybees. This contribution was calculated as per-visit e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness multiplied by abundance for each species; we used functional traits as a proxy for per-visit e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness and then multiplied these trait values by the abundance of the bees. Rather than correlating abundance of wild bees and honeybees to seed set (see Mallinger and Gratton 2015; Martins et al., 2015; Blitzer et al., 2016), we directly quanti<sup>fi</sup>ed their relative contribution to seed set and tested whether this contribution was signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly di<sup>f</sup>erent.

## 2.1. Abundance

We used a subset of the abundance data collated by Russo et al. (2015) for the visitation rate component of the pollinator contribution equation (Ne eman et al., 2010Ne’eman et al., 2010). Bees were net collected in apple orchards during bloom at 28 orchards from 2008 to 2013 (see Russo et al., 2015 for a full description). Over 100 wild bee species were detected in these orchards, but a relatively small number of species made up the vast majority of the abundance. To compare relative pollinator contributions to seed set, we exclusively used abundance data from 2013 bee surveys of orchards (53 species, 99 15- min transects) where seed set monitoring occurred that same year (Blitzer et al., 2016). Once we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed functional traits that contributed measurably to seed set, we compared relative pollinator contribution across orchards (Figs. 1 and 2), using abundance data from 15 min standardized transects (Russo et al., 2015) surveyed in 2011–2013 (78 bee species, 363 transects) because sampling was most consistent during these years. Thus, all abundance data used in this study are from 15-min standardized net collections in NY apple orchards.

Species were assigned to functional groups based on close taxonomic relatedness and similar size and behavior patterns. These functional group classi<sup>fi</sup>cations allowed us to include the entire wild bee community in our investigation because we could measure functional traits on a representative species from each functional group, and then assume these functional traits were similar across the group. Many bee species were represented by a small number of individuals; ignoring these rare species would have underestimated the bee community’s contribution to pollination. We therefore assigned rare species to functional groups that were de<sup>fi</sup>ned by closely related, abundant species, on which we are able to measure functional traits in a replicated fashion. When placed into functional groups, 14 species represented 99% of total functional group diversity (see Table S1 for functional group classi<sup>fi</sup>cation). Below, we describe these measurable functional traits in more detail. In the analyses reported in the main body of thi paper, we use these functional group classi<sup>fi</sup>cations. To ensure that we have not biased our <sup>fi</sup>ndings by doing so, we repeat all of our analyses, in the supplemental materials, with a more conservative model where we only include species on which we directly measured functional traits.

## 2.2. Behavior

Bees that visit <sup>fl</sup>owers speci<sup>fi</sup>cally for nectar can bypass the reproductive parts of the <sup>fl</sup>ower (anthers and stigma) by nectar robbing (Inouye, 1983). In open <sup>fl</sup>owers, such as apple and other Rosaceae, <sup>fl</sup>oral visitors have been observed to visit <sup>fl</sup>owers in two di<sup>f</sup>erent ways. “Side-working” bees are those that land on the petals and probe the base of the anther column without contacting the anthers or stigma (http://tinyurl.com/grvavv3), whereas “top-working” bees actively gather pollen and contact both anthers and stigma (http://tinyurl.com/ zju8v65) (Thomson and Goodell, 2001; Park et al., 2015).

## 2.3. Other functional traits

In addition to <sup>fl</sup>oral handling, we tested two other functional traits: pollen purity and bee body size (Fig. 1). We de<sup>fi</sup>ne pollen purity as the proportion of the pollen load consisting of apple pollen relative to pollen of other species. The pollen counts were conducted on <sup>fi</sup>eld collected specimens from surveyed apple orchards by the authors for the purposes of this study. Our hypothesis was that pollen purity is positively correlated with legitimate pollen deposition (Larsson, 2005). We quanti<sup>fi</sup>ed the proportion of Malus pollen carried by 170 bees of 14 species representing 99% of total functional group diversity (Table S1). We identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the species composition of the pollen, calculated the proportion of Malus pollen, and tested whether this was a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant predictor of orchard level seed set (see statistical analyses below). A full description of the pollen purity analysis is in the supplemental mate rials.

We also hypothesized that larger bees, because of their larger sur face area, will have a higher per-visit e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness than smaller bees. We collected data from the literature on body length of each bee species (Mitchell, 1960) and measured the intertegular distances of the <sup>fi</sup>eldcollected bees for the purposes of this study. As intertegular distances in this case were highly correlated with body length values (see supplemental materials), we found little di<sup>f</sup>erence in the analyses resulting from these two measures. As a result, we used body length for the <sup>fi</sup>nal models reported in this manuscript. The intertegular distances are re ported in the supplemental materials. We test whether body size is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant predictor of seed set in the apple orchards (see statistical analyses below).

## 2.4. Statistical analyses

To test which bee functional traits have a meaningful relationship with the economic value of pollination service, we used seed set as our response variable (Blitzer et al., 2016). Blitzer et al. (2016) monitored fruit and seed set on 6 trees of several di<sup>f</sup>erent varieties at a subset (15 of 23) of orchards surveyed for bee species richness in Russo et al. (2015). We used generalized linear mixed e<sup>f</sup>ects models R package lmer (R Core Team, 2013) to conduct model comparisons with seed set in the surveyed apple orchards as the response variable. We used apple variety and orchard location as random e<sup>f</sup>ects and kept these random e<sup>f</sup>ects constant for all model comparisons. We based all model comparisons o<sup>f</sup> of a basic model from Blitzer et al. (2016), which found a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant positive correlation between seed set and both species richness and abundance of wild bees, but not abundance of honeybees. For our model, we used predictors of honeybee abundance and wild bee abundance. We compared models composed of the abundance of honeybees and wild bees, plus functional traits of these species. Then we compared every model combination of honeybee abundance, wild bee abundance, <sup>fl</sup>ower handling behavior (proportion of top working visits), pollen purity (proportion of Malus pollen), and body size (listed explicitly in Table 2). We used AIC values and percent variation explained to identify the best <sup>fi</sup>t model for this comparison, where the best <sup>fi</sup>t model had the lowest AIC value and the highest percent variation. We divide percent variation explained into marginal $\scriptstyle \mathrm { \mathrm { R } } ^ { 2 }$ and conditional $\mathrm { R } ^ { 2 }$ , where the marginal $\scriptstyle \mathrm { \mathrm { R } } ^ { 2 }$ shows the variation explained by the <sup>fi</sup>xed e<sup>f</sup>ects alone and the conditional $\mathrm { R } ^ { 2 }$ shows the variation explained by both the <sup>fi</sup>xed and random e<sup>f</sup>ects (Johnson, 2014; Nakagawa and Schielzeth, 2013). The model variations we explored, their AIC values, and the percent variation they explain are presented in Table 2.

The <sup>fi</sup>xed e<sup>f</sup>ects of our best model as determined by our model selection process were then considered to be signi<sup>fi</sup>cant contributors to seed set. We directly compared the contributions of wild bees and honeybees to seed set according to these functional traits (signi<sup>fi</sup>cant <sup>fi</sup>xed e<sup>f</sup>ects) by treating signi<sup>fi</sup>cant <sup>fi</sup>xed e<sup>f</sup>ects as proxies for per-visit e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness and multiplying them by species abundance to calculate pollinator contribution. We used abundance data from 2011 to 2013 and included all species represented by a functional group (Table S1).

There was no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant di<sup>f</sup>erence in the abundance of honeybees and wild bees across study orchards $( \mathrm { F i g . 1 A , P = 0 . 0 8 } )$ . Wild bees were equally abundant despite the fact that, of the 23 orchards evaluated, only six did not rent honeybee hives for the duration of the study. There was no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant e<sup>f</sup>ect of honeybee abundance or hive density on wild bee abundance or species richness $( \mathrm { P } \ > \ 0 . 0 5$ , Russo et al., 2015).

## 3.2. Behavior

(2015) and Park et al. (2015). For groups where Martins et al. (2015) and Park et al. (2015) overlapped, we averaged the reported values, which were very similar (Table S1). This proportion varied between 0.54 and 1.00, with honeybees being the least likely to top-work <sup>fl</sup>owers (Table 1). Bees with the highest proportion of top-working individuals (100%) included Bombus, large Andrena, and Colletes inaequalis.

## 3.3. Other functional traits

Compared to honeybees, wild bees carried proportionately more apple pollen and were bigger, on average, per transect across orchards $( \mathbf { P } ~ < ~ 0 . 0 1$ for both, purity t stat = 7.6, size t stat = 8.2, Fig. 1J, H). Neither purity of pollen loads or body size, however, signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly contributed to seed set at the orchard level (Table 2). Compared to the basic model, models containing size alone, purity alone, or the basic model variables (i.e. abundance) with size or purity alone were not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly di<sup>f</sup>erent from the basic model alone (Table 2). The basic model with purity and size was signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly worse than the basic model alone (Table 2). A full model containing all three functional traits and abundance performed signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly better than the basic model alone, but this full model explained less variation and had a higher AIC value than the basic model with <sup>fl</sup>ower handling behavior alone (Table 2). Excluding bees represented by functional groups did not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly change our results (see supplemental materials).

## 4. Discussion

Our study demonstrates that the best predictors of seed set in NY apple orchards are abundance and <sup>fl</sup>ower handling behavior of the bee community. Using these functional traits, we quanti<sup>fi</sup>ed the relative contribution of wild bees relative to honeybees and show that wild bees contribute signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly more than honeybees to seed set across the study area. Here we provide a comprehensive view of mechanisms driving the increased contribution of wild pollinators to seed set in apple orchards, as well as potential insight into the role of wild pollinators in other crops. Our study is in agreement with other empirical studies, showing bee abundance (Olsen, 1996; Winfree et al., 2015) and <sup>fl</sup>ower handling behavior (Park et al., 2015) are important factors in seed set. Because apple orchards are a mass blooming crop with a large number of <sup>fl</sup>owers, it is no surprise that pollinator abundance is a key driver of pollinator contribution to seed set. Theory also supports the importance of abundance in dictating pollinator contributions both in agricultural and natural systems (Ne eman et al., 2010Ne’eman et al., 2010). We have further shown that we can strengthen our understanding of the mechanisms driving the contributions of wild bees to seed set by combining information about <sup>fl</sup>ower handling behavior with abundance.

The functional traits of pollen purity and body size were not signi<sup>fi</sup>cant predictors of seed set at the orchard level, though both may in<sup>fl</sup>uence pollen deposition (see supplemental materials). This may be because at the scale of the orchard, with many thousands of <sup>fl</sup>owers, the abundance of a given pollinator and whether or not it contacts the reproductive parts of the <sup>fl</sup>ower are more essential for pollination e<sup>fi</sup>- cacy. On the other hand, this lack of signi<sup>fi</sup>cance may relate to the fact that the majority of the abundant bees collected in apple orchards were relatively similar in size and carried a similar proportion of Malus pollen. Andrenids were the most abundant wild bees in our study, while these bees vary in size, many are similar in size to a honeybee worker (Table S1). Honeybees and the most abundant Andrena species in our study orchards also carried similarly high proportions of Malus pollen (Fig. S1).

The importance of a particular functional trait for crop pollination may change over space and time. The seed set data used in this study were collected over the course of one year (Blitzer et al., 2016). Because we surveyed the bee communities in these same orchards that year, we were able to directly compare the composition of the bee community with the ultimate seed set. However, the abundance and composition of the bee community <sup>fl</sup>uctuates greatly from year to year (Russo et al., 2015). Thus, it is possible that other functional traits of these pollinators will contribute more to seed set in other years. For example, though body size was not a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant predictor of seed set in our study, this trait may become important in years where the community composition of bees signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly shifts toward larger or smaller bees. Likewise, the majority of bees in our study carried relatively high proportions of Malus pollen. If the composition of the bee community shifts toward bees that rarely visit Malus, pollen purity may become a much more signi<sup>fi</sup>cant trait in determining seed set. For example, Park et al. (2015) showed that the relationship between pollen grains deposited and likelihood of fruit set was a saturating curve. Other factors not measured in this study (e.g., visitation rate of di<sup>f</sup>erent bee species) may also play a role in pollination contribution and are potential avenues fo future research.

Honeybees have an unusually high propensity to side-work <sup>fl</sup>owers in comparison to wild bees and the main e<sup>f</sup>ect of this behavior was to reduce the contribution of honeybees. Honeybees have greater nectar requirements to build honey stores; as a result, honeybee workers may specialize on nectar rewards and use side-working as an e<sup>fi</sup>cient method of <sup>fl</sup>oral handling. The values describing the <sup>fl</sup>ower handling behavior of bees observed visiting apple <sup>fl</sup>owers were limited; most wild bees exhibited a high proportion of top-working visits, while honeybees only contacted the reproductive parts of 50% of <sup>fl</sup>owers visited (Park et al., 2015; Martins et al., 2015). In spite of this limitation, <sup>fl</sup>ower handling behavior was a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant predictor of seed set in apple orchards (Table 2). This suggests that bees that do not contact the stigma of the <sup>fl</sup>ower directly are unlikely to transfer enough viable pollen for fertilization to occur (but see Degrandi-Ho<sup>f</sup>man et al., 1985). This may explain why Blitzer et al. (2016) found no correlation between honeybee abundance and seed set at the orchard level. Flower handling is also important for the pollination of buzz-pollinated crops, such as tomatoes (Banda and Paxton, 1990) and blueberries (Javorek et al., 2002). However, in crops with very small <sup>fl</sup>owers in which most <sup>fl</sup>oral visitors always make contact with the anthers and stigma, <sup>fl</sup>ower handling may be irrelevant to pollinator e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness.

Our study provides some insights into quantifying the seed set contribution of a community of wild bees relative to that of the honeybee. While per-visit pollen deposition is often thought to be the most accurate measure of per-visit e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness, we <sup>fi</sup>nd the <sup>fl</sup>ower handling behavior (proportion of top-working visits) of bees was a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant predictor of seed set in apples. Future studies of pollinator contribution in crops with a diverse wild bee fauna might focus on quantifying <sup>fl</sup>ower handling behavior as a potential proxy for per-visit e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness, given the challenges of obtaining per-visit pollen deposition data for large numbers of species. On the other hand, the labor intensive process of identifying the composition of pollen loads carried by bees may not be worthwhile for quantifying pollinator contributions to crops. While increased pollen purity did increase seed set at the scale of an individual apple <sup>fl</sup>ower (Park et al., 2015), it was not a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant factor at the scale of the orchard. This may be due to the fact that orchards in full bloom can have trillions of <sup>fl</sup>owers and, at this scale, the sheer quantity of visits is more important than the quality of any individual visit.

Finally, no matter how we quantify pollinator contribution, unmanaged bees, especially of the genus Andrena, are providing critical pollination services in New York apple orchards. When we quantify the contribution of pollinators to seed set as <sup>fl</sup>ower handling behavior and abundance, wild bees contribute more than honeybees in 17 of 23 orchards and an equal amount in an additional 4 orchards, despite the fact that only 6 of the orchards did not rent honeybee hives during the study (Fig. 2). When we quantify pollinator contribution in this way, wild bees are more than twice as important in apple orchards as hon eybees (Fig. 1B).

## 5. Conclusion

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings support the use of pollinator abundance as a proxy for pollinator contribution to crop yield (e.g. Garibaldi et al., 2013; Vazquez et al., 2005), but demonstrate <sup>fl</sup>ower handling behavior is also an important component of pollinator services. Understanding per-visit pollen deposition, or the purity of pollen loads carried by bees might be less important than bee <sup>fl</sup>ower handling behavior and abundance for agricultural pollination. The importance of <sup>fl</sup>ower handling behavio has been shown in crops that must be buzz-pollinated, such as blueberries (Javorek et al., 2002), and where <sup>fl</sup>owers are di<sup>fi</sup>cult for bees to handle, such as alfalfa (Cane, 2002). In apples, this means the visitation rate of honeybees is e<sup>f</sup>ectively cut in half, as they only contact the stigma and anthers of <sup>fl</sup>owers approximately half of the time. Wild bees in apple orchards are more likely to contact the reproductive parts of the <sup>fl</sup>ower on each visit, and thus contribute more in terms of pollination services where they are abundant. Such trends are likely to be found in other crop systems where the <sup>fl</sup>ower morphology facilitates honeybee side-working (e.g. peaches, plums, cherries, apricots, and almonds). Our study helps explain recent research demonstrating a strong relationship between yield and wild bee abundance, but not honeybee abundance (e.g. Garibaldi et al., 2013; Mallinger and Gratton, 2015). Ultimately, understanding the mechanisms driving speci<sup>fi</sup>c pollinator contributions to crop yield can help us manage for more effective pollination services. In the case of New York apples, given the e<sup>fi</sup>cacy of the wild bees, it might be more cost-e<sup>f</sup>ective for some apple growers to develop strategies to support a healthy wild bee community than to rent additional honeybee hives.

We identified three traits we felt could directly or indirectly impact per-visit pollen deposition: pollen purity, bee body size, and flower handling behavior. Here in the supplemental materials, we provide a more thorough explanation of pollen purity and bee body size, as well as the relationship between purity, size, and behavior with pollen deposition.

Pollen purity

We define pollen purity as the proportion of the pollen load consisting of apple pollen relative to pollen of other species. Previous studies (e.g. (Sipes and Tepedino, 2005) have defined a pure pollen load as >90% of a single pollen type. Our hypothesis is that pollen purity will be positively correlated with pollen deposition (Larsson, 2005) and we test this by analyzing the pollen carried by 170 bees of 14 bee species and comparing it to a published dataset on pollen deposition in the same system (Park et al., 2015). The 14 species we analyzed represented 99% of total bee functional group diversity (see Table S1 for functional group classification).

We used the methods described in (Edens-Meier et al., 2011) to wash pollen off of net-collected bee specimens (see also (Bernhardt, 1987). The pollen was washed off of bee specimens onto microscope slides, where we counted at least 300 pollen grains from ten randomly selected individuals of each of 14 bee species. Together, these species comprised 85% of the total bee abundance found in the apple orchards during our study. The pollen counts involved at least 3 random transects across the slide for each individual. Pollen grains were photographed, measured, and categorized using a reference library of pollen collected from all flowering angiosperms at the study sites in 2014 (http://blogs.cornell.edu/pollengrains/), as well as several reference books (Crompton and Wojtas, 1993; Kapp et al., 2000; Lewis et al., 1983). Malus pollen was identified based on size, shape, surface sculpturing, and the presence/absence of similar species based on extensive floral surveys (Russo unpublished data).

We quantified percent Malus pollen (pollen purity from here on) for each species as the number of Malus pollen grains divided by the total number of pollen grains counted for each individual. Pollen purity is evaluated for its relationship with pollen deposition and seed set in the orchards (see below).

Male bees comprised approximately 12% of the overall bee abundance in the study. For most of the species, females outnumber males, but males outnumbered females significantly in Andrena crataegi, A. imitatrix, and Xylocopa virginica (Table S1). For this reason, we also counted pollen grains on male A. crataegi and X. virginica (these two species alone comprise 62% of male abundance). If we leave out all bee species for which pollen purity was not assessed directly (i.e. species represented by functional groups) we counted pollen on roughly 83% of total bee abundance. To quantify the services provided by the wild bee community as a whole, we represented species for which we did not measure pollen purity directly by using measured values from species in the same subgenus (placing them in taxonomically and morphologically based functional groups). In comparing our model results with and without the species we did not directly measure, we found the strongest models include functional group representation (Table 2).

Bees carried between 28% (Lasioglossum hitchensi) and 80% (Andrena miserabilis) Malus pollen, on average (Fig. S1, Table 1). While carrying less pollen, males of both Xylocopa virginica and Andrena crataegi had higher average pollen purity than their female counterparts. When we calculated pollinator importance as the pollen purity multiplied by the abundance of each bee species, wild bees were found to be more important than honeybees across all the orchards, on average, but this difference was not significant (P > 0.05, Fig. 2C).

Body size

Attributes of bee morphology might contribute to the amount of pollen deposited per visit. Mean body size varies widely among bee species and could thus affect relative pollen deposition. We hypothesize that larger bees, because of their larger surface area, will deposit more pollen per visit than smaller bees. We test this by comparing body size data (bee length and intertegular distance) from field collected specimens to a pollen deposition study in the same system (Park et al., 2015).

We used the information on bee lengths available in Mitchell (Mitchell, 1962) for all the male and female bee species present in our study. When a range was provided, we used average bee length as a measure of size. In addition, we measured intertegular distances for the male and female bees of the species represented in our study (Table S1).

The length of bees in this study varied between 4 and 22 mm, with an average size of 9.93 mm. The ten most abundant bee species had an average size of 12.07 mm (Supplemental Table 1). When bee length was multiplied by abundance across all species, wild bees were found to be more important than honeybees on average, but this difference was not significant (P > 0.05, Fig. 2D). The intertegular distances of bees varied between 0.81 and 6.41 mm, with an average of 2.18. The intertegular distance correlated strongly with bee length (0.88, P < 0.01, Fig. S2), and behaved similarly in all analyses; thus, the results based on intertegular distances are presented in the supplemental materials.

Relationship between pollen purity, size, and behavior with per-visit pollen deposition

To test which factors are good proxies for the pollen bees deposit on flower stigmas, we compared our data to per-visit pollen deposition data from a recent study by Park et al. (2015). Park et al. (2015) conducted a study of pollen deposition in apple orchards involving three different bee species. First, they interviewed individual bees to quantify the number of pollen grains deposited on apple stigmas after a single visit by bees of the subgenus Melandrena, bees of the genus Bombus, or Apis mellifera (see Park et al. 2015 for further details). They also captured bees, chilled them, and applied either the scopa or the thorax of the bees to stigmas and then allowed the fruit to develop. This second part of the study was conducted because flowers are removed from stems in the interview method and it is not possible to compare pollen deposition to fruit set using that methodology. Park et al. (2015) used chilled bees of Andrena subgenus Melandrena (comprising several, closely related species), Apis mellifera, and Bombus impatiens. They found a strong, positive correlation between pollen deposition and fruit set (see Park et al. 2015). The authors also have unpublished data on the pollen deposition of interviewed Xylocopa virginica, and chilled Andrena nasonii (Park, unpub data), which we also compare here to our measures of pollen purity.

Our results suggest the pollen purity of the bees does relate to the pollen deposited on the stigmas, but that body size mediates this relationship in the field. For example, there was a direct correlation between pollen purity and pollen deposition for chilled bees applied to stigmas (estimate 1.6, P < 0.001, Fig. S2). However, for live interviewed bees, pollen deposition was positively correlated with the product of bee size and purity (estimate 103.25, P < 0.01, Fig. S2), although neither pollen purity nor bee size alone predicted pollen deposition.

In addition to the individual contributions of these factors, they might interact in important ways. For example, the relative contribution of these two components might be driven by scale. One might expect that per-visit effectiveness is more critical at the scale of an individual flower (Larsson, 2005), while abundance dominates at larger scales (Olsen, 1996; Winfree et al., 2015).

At the scale of an individual flower, the combined results of Park et al. (2015) and the pollen purity analysis show that pollen deposition is correlated with pollen purity when chilled bees are applied to the stigma of apple flowers, but that this effect is mediated by bee size in live foraging bees. Thus, pollen purity is not an important factor at the scale of the entire orchard. The distinction between pollinator importance at these two different scales is important for understanding how factors that increase the probability of a single flower setting fruit relate to factors that increase orchard-level yield. Our results demonstrate that these factors are not necessarily the same.
