## Abstract

Understanding species adaptations to human-modified ecosystems is central to evolutionary and conservation biology. It is essential to evaluate trait variations in populations that occur along environmental gradients to understand the adaptive potential of species to human-driven environmental change. We analyzed morphological trait variations in the workers of four common and phylogenetically distant ant species (Dinoponera quadriceps, Dorymyrmex goeldii, Pheidole radoszkowskii, and Ectatomma muticum) along disturbance and aridity gradients in the Caatinga dry forest. Ants were collected using pitfall traps in 20 0.1-ha plots distributed over an area of 213.75 km<sup>2</sup>. Up to five individuals per species were selected from each plot to measure eight morphological traits. Between-plot variability contributed considerably (\~37%) to the total variability of morphological traits. This pattern of high between-plot variability was consistent across species and traits. Such morphological intraspecific variations between plots were not strongly related to the aridity or disturbance gradients. However, when ant morphology did respond to environmental change, the response was stronger to aridity rather than disturbance, and responses were most conspicuous in P. radoskowskii. Our results suggest that ants in the Caatinga may have substantial potential for morphological adaptation to environmental changes and that disturbance intensity and variations in aridity are not strong determinants of ant worker morphology despite influencing morphological variation over small geographical distances. These morphological variations are likely to represent key assets, by enabling individuals and population to inhabit the variety of habitats provided by Caatinga and to cope with upcoming changes in land use and climate.

## 1  |  INTRODUCTION

Understanding species adaptations to ecosystems altered by human activity is fundamental to evolutionary and conservation biology. For species to persist in remaining disturbed habitats, they must have suitable physiological, morphological, behavioral, and life history functional traits for the environmental filters imposed by human impacts (Southwood, 1988; Violle et al., 2007); have plasticity in the expression of their traits (Beever et al., 2017; Hadfield & Strathmann, 1996); or adapt to new environmental conditions through natural selection (Hadfield & Strathmann, 1996). Morphological traits are widely used to assess species responses in relation to environmental gradients (Arnan et al., 2012; Gibb & Parr, 2013; Pigot et al., 2016; Sfair et al., 2018). Since body size is strongly related to organism physiology (Peters, 1983) and resource exploitation (Okuzaki et al. 2009, Stouffer et al., 2011), body size is the most used morphological measure to assess species’ responses to environmental gradients. For instance, according to Bergmann's rule, individuals from the same species tend to be larger in cooler environments (Bergmann, 1847; Blackburn et al., 1999; Weeks et al., 2020). Other morphological traits can also be important for explaining species adaptations to environmental change. For instance, the size-grain hypothesis (Kaspari & Weiser, 1999) states that species with smaller legs can take advantage of more complex environments, making it easier to move between cracks, and such effects might also translate to the population level (Chown & Nicolson, 2004).

Ants are a suitable model system to analyzing morphological variation along environmental gradients because they exhibit high levels of morphological and functional variation, in addition to being extremely sensitive to environmental change (Andersen & Majer, 2004; Underwood & Fisher, 2006), especially in the tropics (Hölldobler & Wilson, 1990). During the last decade, a large and growing literature of morphological trait-based analyses on worker ants along environmental gradients has been published (e.gBihn et al., 2010; Gibb et al., 2018; Parr et al., 2017; Silva & Brandão, 2010; Yates et al., 2014) including studies linking morphological traits and ecological functions (Bihn et al., 2010; Kaspari & Weiser, 1999; Parr et al., 2017). These studies focus on average species trait values and assume that intraspecific variation is small or negligible. Thus, we still know little about morphological changes at the population level in ants. A recent study found very low levels of intraspecific variation along an elevation gradient (1–4% of total trait variation), probably masked by the large variation among species that accounted for most of the total variation (Gaudard et al., 2019). Moreover, Gibb et al. (2018) suggest that recent evolutionary pressures on ants can generate many relationships between morphological traits and the environment. Furthermore, recent studies have demonstrated that workers of Leptothorax acervorum and Aphaenogaster senilis populations are larger at higher altitudes (Bernadou et al., 2016; Shik et al., 2019).

Here, we analyze the variation in eight morphological traits of four common and phylogenetically distant ant species, and test whether this variation responds to disturbance and aridity gradients in the Caatinga dry forest. The Caatinga is one of the world's most highly populated and threatened semi-arid biomes, sustaining

28 million people who are highly dependent on forest natural resources for their livelihoods in a typical regime of chronic anthropogenic disturbance (Silva et al., 2017). In addition, Caatinga is highly threatened by climate change, with climate models forecasting a 22– 40% reduction in rainfall and a 3–6°C increase in temperatures by 2100 (Magrin et al., 2014). Increasing aridity has been observed even within small geographic ranges (Rito et al., 2017). Thus, Caatinga offers an excellent opportunity to study potential morphological adaptations to disturbance and climate change. Moreover, previous research on the Caatinga has shown that chronic anthropogenic disturbance and/or aridity do not affect ant abundance or species richness, but does alter species composition (Arnan, Arcoverde, et al., 2018; Câmara et al., 2018; Oliveira et al., 2017) and spatial–temporal foraging activity of widely distributed species (Silva et al., 2019). Despite this, many species are unaffected and occur along a large portion of the disturbance and/or aridity gradients. Morphological traits may play an important role in how ant populations respond to increases in chronic disturbance and aridity gradients.

Our specific aims are: (1) to characterize and quantify the levels of morphological intraspecific variation within and between study plots throughout the region, (2) to verify to what extent morphological intraspecific variation depends on species or morphological trait identity, and (3) to test to what extent morphological intraspecific variation responds to changes in disturbance and aridity. We predict that: (1) the degree of morphological intraspecific variation will depend on species or morphological trait identity, as previous studies have suggested that some ant clades exhibit greater trait conservatism than others (Hölldobler & Wilson, 1990; Silva & Brandão, 2010; Weiser & Kaspari, 2006), and some traits tend to be more plastic than others (Gaudard et al., 2019; Oms et al., 2017; Purcell et al., 2016; Yates et al., 2014); and (2) morphological intraspecific variation will respond to changes in disturbance and aridity, as they are the strongest environmental filters in the study area (Arnan, Arcoverde, et al., 2018; Rito et al., 2017).

## 2  |  MATERIAL AND METHODS

## 2.1  |  Study area

The study was carried out in Catimbau National Park (8º24′ 00″-8º36′ 35″S; 37º09′ 30″-37º14′ 40″W), a 607-km<sup>2</sup> protected area located in the state of Pernambuco, northeastern Brazil (Figure S1). The climate is semi-arid, with annual temperature averaging 23°C, and mean annual rainfall of 600 mm, concentrated between March and July. Approximately 70% of the park area is covered by quartzite sandy soils, but planosols (15%) and lithosols (15%) are also present. The vegetation is composed of shrubs and small trees up to 7 m in height and is dominated by the Leguminosae, Euphorbiaceae, and Myrtaceae families (Rito et al., 2017). Despite the park being established in 2002, local inhabitants still remain within its borders; therefore, it continues to be subject to a regime of chronic anthropogenic disturbance, including livestock grazing, timber harvesting, firewood collection, and hunting (Arnan, Leal, et al., 2018; Rito et al., 2017). Catimbau includes a very steep rainfall gradient from 1100 mm in the south-east to 480 mm in the northwest (Rito et al., 2017); such bioclimatic gradients provide powerful frameworks for addressing climate change impacts through space-for-time substitutions (Blois et al., 2013; Caddy-Retalic et al., 2017). We sampled twenty permanent plots (Figure S1) measuring 20 m × 50 m (0.1 ha) that were established for several studies carried out as part of the Long-Term Ecological Research Project PELD-Catimbau (www.peldcatimbau.org); they are separated by at least 2 km within a total area of 213.75 km<sup>2</sup> (Rito et al., 2017).

## 2.2  |  Characterizing chronic anthropogenic disturbance and aridity gradients

To assess the effects of disturbance on the variability of ant morphology, we used a global multi-metric disturbance index previously established for the study plots (Arnan, Leal, et al., 2018). We integrated into a single index nine disturbance metrics that are related to the three main sources of chronic anthropogenic disturbance in the Caatinga: livestock grazing, wood extraction, and exploitation of nontimber forest products (Arnan, Leal, et al., 2018; Rito et al., 2017). For livestock grazing, we conducted field measurements of goat-trail length and density of goat and cattle dung. For wood extraction, we measured biomass of live stems and coarse woody debris in the field. For exploitation of non-timber products, we used four surrogates of proximate human pressure: proximity to the nearest house, proximity to the nearest village, proximity to the nearest road (using satellite imagery in ArcGIS 10.1 software), and number of people in the nearest village (obtained by interviewing local households) with influence weighted by distance. The values for each disturbance metric were first standardized between 0 and 1 to make component metrics of equal importance. We then computed the global multi-metric index by summing the three components and multiplying by 100. The overall index can potentially vary from 0 (zero values for all component metrics) to 100 (maximum values of all component disturbance metrics). However, it ranged from 2 to 58 in our study area. Full details on this disturbance index are provided in Arnan, Leal, et al. (2018).

We obtained water deficit/aridity data from hydrological maps of the CGIAR-CSI's Global Aridity and PET Database and Global High-Resolution Soil Water Database (Trabucco & Zomer, 2010). These maps have a 30-sec resolution, equivalent to 1 km. To plot these data onto maps, the annual average water deficit was calculated from the difference between the mean annual potential evapotranspiration (PET) and the actual evapotranspiration (AET) using the ArcGIS 10.1 software. Water deficit ranged between 658 mm and 1086 mm.

## 2.3  |  Ant sampling

In each of the 20 plots, we established 20 pitfall traps (in a 4 × 5 grid) positioned 5 m apart. Traps were constructed from 12-cm-diameter plastic pots partially filled with an alcohol and ethylene glycol solution with detergent to preserve the sample and were activated for 48 h in March 2015 (for more details on ant sampling, see Arnan, Arcoverde, et al., 2018). We selected the four most widely distributed species (i.e., species occurring in ≥10 plots) in our study landscape to measure morphological attributes, which belonged to four different subfamilies following an evolutionary gradient from the closest to the baseline to the most evolutionary recent species (Dinoponera quadriceps (Ponerinae), Dorymyrmex goeldii (Dolichoderinae), Pheidole radoszkowskii (Myrmicinae), and Ectatomma muticum (Ectatomminae)) (Moreau & Bell, 2013). These species also follow a gradient of worker size, with D. quadriceps displaying the largest workers, P. radoskowskii the smallest workers, and E. muticum and D. goeldii exhibiting intermediate sized workers. For each species and plot, we measured between 3 and 10 (median = 5) randomly selected workers from the captured individuals. A recent study showed that, on average, measuring around six individual worker ants is enough to generate a trait mean close to the population mean (Gaudard et al., 2019). Moreover, many studies and databases of ant traits use between three and 10 individuals (Bishop et al., 2015; Gibb et al., 2014; Parr et al., 2017; Silva & Brandão, 2010). Out of the 20 plots, we could only collect between 3 and 5 individuals in 14 plots for D. quadriceps, 13 plots for E. muticum and P. radoszkowskii, and 10 plots for D. goeldii. The plots where each species occurred were well distributed along both our chronic anthropogenic disturbance and aridity gradients, except for Dormyrmex goeldii that only occurred in the most arid plots (Figure S2).

## 2.4  |  Morphological traits

For each selected individual, we measured eight morphological traits that may respond to environmental changes and that are particularly sensitive to variations in aridity and chronic anthropogenic disturbances (Table 1). For each individual, we measured body size (Weber's length), eye length, antenna (scape + funicle) length, mandible length, clypeus length, leg (tibia +femur) length, inter-ocular distance, and pilosity (Table 1). These traits reflect body size, foraging capacity, foraging period, and resource acquisition strategy and can respond to environmental change (Bihn et al., 2010; Parr et al., 2017). We standardized all trait measurements (except Weber's length and pilosity) by dividing each by the Weber's length to limit correlations with body size. We then analyzed Weber's length, eye length ratio, antenna length ratio, mandible length ratio, clypeus length ratio, leg length ratio, inter-ocular distance ratio, and pilosity. Pheidole radoskowskii possesses minor and major worker castes; only minor workers were measured. Pilosity was not measured for Dorymyrmex goeldii due to the absence of hairs in this species. Overall, 1934 trait measurements were taken.

## 2.5  |  Statistical analyses

To understand the degree and source of variability in ant morphological traits, we performed one linear model per morphological trait and species, where trait value was the response variable and plot was the fixed factor. Then, we computed the between-plot variability based on the Sum of squares (Sum Sq) of the plot factor and of the residuals from the model as follows:

% = [Sum Sq Plot / (Sum Sq Plot + Sum Sq Residuals)]\*100. The within-plot variability was computed as: $\% _ { \mathrm { w i t h i n } } = 1 0 0 \cdot \% _ { \mathrm { b e t w e e n } } .$ We then analyzed differences in the variability between plots among species and traits by means of a two-way ANOVA.

To characterize the overall variability in ant morphology between plots along the disturbance and aridity gradients, we first used a multivariate approach, performing a principal component analysis (PCA) of ant morphological traits for each ant species. All ant morphological traits were standardized prior to the analysis. Environmental variables (aridity and disturbance) were also plotted as supplementary variables in bi-dimensional PCA space (Legendre and Legendre, 1998). We then computed the mean and the variance for each morphological trait, species, and plot, and constructed statistical models to test the effect of disturbance and aridity on variability in ant morphology (mean and variance) for each trait and species separately. We employed Akaike's information criterion with a correction for finite sample sizes (AICc) to select the best-supported models. This approach reduces the problems associated with multiple testing, co-linearity of explanatory variables, and small sample sizes (Burnham and Anderson, 2002). ## 3  |  RESULTS

Intraspecific variability within plots made the largest contribution to total variation for most morphological traits and species, but intraspecific variation between plots was considerable (Figure 1). The mean (±SE) contribution of between-plot variability to total variability was $3 7 \pm 3 \%$ (range: 16.7–85.6%) and did not differ among species $( \mathsf { A N O V A } ; F _ { 3 , 2 7 } = 0 . 2 , p = 0 . 8 6 1 )$ or morphological traits (ANOVA; $F _ { 7 , 2 3 } = 1 . 9 , p = 0 . 1 3 0 )$ . The lowest values of between-plot variability (<20%) were those of the mandible length ratio in E. muticum, the leg length ratio in E. muticum and D. goeldii, and the inter-ocular distance in D. goeldii (Figure 1). The highest values of between-plot variability ( 70%) were for clypeus length ratio in E. muticum and D. quadriceps (Figure 1). In general, most traits were not correlated with each other, and when they were, most were not highly correlated (i.e., $r < 0 . 7 0 )$ (Figure S3). Out of the 28 possible correlation coefficients for D. quadriceps and E. muticum, 10 were significant for D. quadriceps and 15 for E. muticum. However, only antenna length ratio and Weber's length were highly correlated $( r > 0 . 7 0 )$ in both species. P. radoskowiskii was the ant species with fewest traits significantly correlated with each other (6 out of 28), and none of the correlation coefficients were greater than 0.70. For D. goeldii, out of the 21 possible correlation coefficients, six were significant and none of them were greater than 0.70 (Figure S3).

## 4  |  DISCUSSION

In this study, we report important morphological variation in the populations of four phylogenetically distant ant species over a small spatial (geographic) area in the Caatinga. Morphological variation is similar across ant species and traits and does not seem to be strongly related to either disturbance or aridity. However, the proportion of morphological variation that is related to environmental change is most strongly associated with changes in aridity, especially in populations of Pheidole radoskowskii and a subset of morphological traits we studied. These results indicate the adaptation potential of ants for dealing with future changes in land use and climate (e.g., increased aridity).

![](images/9a9778ca4075e1f5d5355326f9192f042c4efa64d0b40b36070217909ea5e7f9.jpg)  
F I G U R E 6  Linear relationship between variance in pilosity of Ectatomma muticum and disturbance in the Catimbau National Park, northeastern Brazil

The contribution of between-plot variability ( 37%) to total morphological variability found here is in line with several studies on plants (Jung et al., 2010; Kazakou et al., 2014; Messier et al., 2010; Sfair et al., 2018), but differs to those reported for invertebrates, in which they display low levels of intraspecific trait variation, as found for dung beetles (3–5%, Griffiths et al., 2016) and ants (1–4%, Gaudard et al., 2019). However, while these studies analyzed a large number of species, we assessed only four ant species. Perhaps the use of the most widely distributed ant species along the gradients contributed to high between-plot intraspecific variation, as previously demonstrated for plants (Sfair et al., 2018). Species presenting a wide distribution must cope with a greater range of environmental constraints (Sanaphre-Villanueva et al., 2016; Sfair et al., 2018). Moreover, our four species may be considered generalists (Hölldobler & Wilson, 1990; Leal et al., 2017) and the similar high morphological variation shared by these species may be a mechanism that allows them to maintain populations in different environmental conditions.

Previous studies have shown that morphology (Silva & Brandão, 2010; Weiser & Kaspari, 2006) and other life history traits (Hölldobler & Wilson, 1990) are often conserved across subfamilies and genera. However, more recent studies demonstrate that most analyzed ant morphological traits are phylogenetically independent (Gaudard et al., 2019; Yates et al., 2014). Nevertheless, since we only analyzed four species, we cannot draw strong conclusions regarding evolutionary causes for the similar levels of morphological variation across the species and traits we observed.

Stronger effects of aridity compared to disturbance have previ ously been shown for dry forests, and this is likely because water is the main limiting factor in these ecosystems (Maass, 1995). Previous studies in the Caatinga dry forest have reported a similar pattern, albeit with other diversity measures such as the taxonomic diversity of plants (Rito et al., 2017), functional and phylogenetic diversity of ants (Arnan, Arcoverde, et al., 2018), and ant–plant interactions (Câmara et al., 2018; Oliveira et al., 2019, 2020). In a sense, aridity seems to impose a higher selective pressure than disturbance at different levels of biological organization. At the species-level, there are some species, such as P. radoskowskii, whose abundance does not respond greatly to increases in either aridity or disturbance (Oliveira et al., 2019), but that might adapt to new conditions through morphological adaptations (as we find here), which are strongly associated with physiology (Peters, 1983). Although D. quadriceps shows some morphological adaptations, it does not seem well adapted to increasing aridity as its populations declined along our aridity gradient (Oliveira et al., 2019). The other two focal species, D. goeldi and E. muticum, did not exhibit considerable changes in their abundances or morphology along the environmental gradients (Oliveira et al., 2019), but they can adapt behaviorally to increasing disturbance and aridity, shifting their foraging from sunny to shaded microhabitats, thus allowing their persistence in increasingly disturbed habitats (Silva et al., 2019). In fact, many ants use behavioral mechanisms to escape or avoid unfavorable conditions (Silva et al., 2019). Exploring the relevance of such mechanisms was beyond the scope of this study but is a potentially interesting avenue for future research on this topic.

In synthesis, the Caatinga dry forest supports a subset of ant fauna with high intraspecific morphological variability, which is in part associated with environmental gradients, particularly aridity, but also chronic anthropogenic disturbance. Such morphological variability is likely to represent a key asset by enabling individuals and populations to inhabit the immense variety of habitat provided by the Caatinga at the landscape and regional level. We refer to species with a high degree of generalist behavior relative to habitat use, food exploitation, and nesting (Hölldobler & Wilson, 1990; Leal et al., 2017), with potential impacts on community organization and structure, as well as the provision of ecosystem functions such as seed dispersal and plant protection against herbivores (Leal et al., 2017; Oliveira et al., 2019, 2020). This morphological variability may also represent a strategic asset for dealing with future changes in land use (i.e., land use intensification) and climate (increased aridity). Considering both the theoretical and applied implications of these findings, future studies should investigate the generality of high variability in Caatinga ant fauna, but also in the fauna of other dry forests, as all such biotas are experiencing changes in land use and climate.
