# Phenological responses to climate warming in temperate moths and butterflies: species traits predict future changes in voltinism

**Tiit Teder**

T. Teder (https://orcid.org/0000-0001-6587-9325) ✉ (tiit.teder@ut.ee), Dept of Zoology, Inst. of Ecology and Earth Sciences, Univ. of Tartu, Vanemuise 46, EE-51003 Tartu, Estonia, and: Faculty of Environmental Sciences, Czech Univ. of Life Sciences Prague, Kamýcká 129, Praha – Suchdol 165 00, Czech Republic.

## Oikos

**129: 1051–1060, 2020**

[doi: 10.1111/oik.07119](http://dx.doi.org/10.1111/oik.07119)

Subject Editor: Matthew Symonds

Editor-in-Chief: Dries Bonte

Accepted 5 March 2020

Changes in the number of generations per year (voltinism) have been among the most common phenological responses to climate warming in insects inhabiting seasonal environments. Nevertheless, numerous species have maintained univoltine (one generation per year) phenology with increasing temperatures, indicating the involvement of phylogenetic, ecological or some other constraints on phenological change. I examined geographic variation in voltinism in moths and butterflies of northern Europe to identify species traits that might predispose species to univoltine/multivoltine phenology. I focused on species with a wide latitudinal distribution range (15 degrees as a minimum) which makes it unlikely that constraints imposed by season length could preclude multivoltinism across their distribution. Almost half of the 731 moth and butterfly species considered appear to have a single generation throughout their entire European range. A univoltine life-cycle across a wide latitudinal gradient suggests the presence of some constraint that makes additional generations either impossible or at least strongly disadvantageous, which will unlikely change with future climate warming. The scattered distribution of univoltine and multivoltine species across the lepidopteran phylogeny indicates that phylogenetic constraints do not strongly limit changes in voltinism, and the trait is open to ecologically-driven adaptive evolution. My data show that species with one generation per year are generally larger than multivoltine species, but size forms no absolute constraint to having multiple generations per year. Obligately univoltine species dominate among egg and adult overwinterers (life-histories typical of so-called spring-feeders), whereas species with capacity for multiple generations prevail among pupal overwinterers. Multivoltinism is also infrequent among species feeding on grasses, particularly in endophagous grass-feeders. Larval diet breadth has no discernible effect on voltinism. Given the diverse ecological consequences of voltinism and its changes, accounting for the species’ capacity for multivoltinism may be a key to address future challenges in biodiversity conservation and pest management.

Keywords: body size, diapause, functional trait, latitudinal variation, phenology, voltinism

## Introduction

The effects of ongoing climate warming range from shifts in species distribution (Parmesan et al. 1999) to changes in body size of the organisms (Gardner et al. 2011), with diverse consequences for populations, species, communities and ecosystems (Carter et al. 2018). Changes in species’ phenologies are among the best documented impacts of climate warming (Parmesan 2006, 2007, Cohen et al. 2018, Kharouba et al. 2018, Duchenne  et  al. 2020). In short-lived organisms, such as insects and many other ectotherms, seasonally earlier start of development and activity, temporal mismatches in consumer–resource interactions, and increased number of generations per year have been the most reported phenological responses (Altermatt 2010a, Diamond  et  al. 2011, Pöyry  et  al. 2011, Roy  et  al. 2015, Kharouba  et  al. 2018, Visser and Gienapp 2019). Nevertheless, phenological sensitivity to climate change strongly varies among species (Primack et al. 2009, Altermatt 2010b, Valtonen et al. 2011, Kharouba et al. 2014, Végvári et al. 2015, Fric et al. 2020). As phenology affects most aspects of species’ ecology (Forrest and Miller-Rushing 2010), identifying traits that associate with phenological sensitivity is necessary for making predictions about species responses to future climate changes.

In temperate environments, the length of the period with favourable ambient temperatures sets the limits for ectotherm growth and development, including the number of generations a species can potentially complete within a season. Consistently, the number of generations per year (= voltinism) typically decreases towards higher latitudes in species capable of multiple generations (Danilevsky  et  al. 1970, Roff 1980, Tauber et al. 1986, Zeuss et al. 2017). In insects, northern populations of many species have shifted from univoltine (i.e. one generation per year) to multivoltine (two or more generations per year) phenologies as a response to recent climate warming (Altermatt 2010a, Pöyry  et  al. 2011). Altermatt (2010b) has shown that shifts in voltinism in response to past temperature increases have occurred in a somewhat predictable manner, with certain traits predisposing species to an increase in voltinism. However, for the overwhelming majority of such species, the increase in voltinism is not unexpected as they have already been regularly multivoltine at lower latitudes of their distribution ranges. The transition area from a univoltine to a multivoltine life cycle in these species has just shifted towards higher latitudes with increasing temperatures. In such species, winter diapause is typically facultative, evolved to survive periods unfavourable for growth, development and reproduction (Danilevsky et al. 1970).

Nevertheless, numerous insect species, even those widely distributed across latitudes, have maintained a univoltine phenology throughout their ranges. As time constraints on development, imposed by seasonality, should be much more relaxed at lower latitudes of species’ distribution ranges, season length is hardly a decisive factor in limiting voltinism in such species (Tammaru  et  al. 2001). This leads to the question of what species traits preclude producing multiple

generations per season. As species that are able to increase the number of seasonal generations have been suggested to become also more abundant with ongoing climate warming (Tobin et al. 2014, Macgregor et al. 2019), identifying ‘truly univoltine and potentially multivoltine species is also important from the perspectives of species conservation and pest control.

In this study, I analyse voltinism in European moths and butterflies to identify traits that associate with producing multiple generations. I use space-for-time substitution approach that is a widely used practice to infer future changes from contemporary spatial patterns (Hodgson  et  al. 2011, Blois et al. 2013). In particular, as the magnitude of ongoing climate warming has not necessarily been sufficient to identify obligately univoltine species, I rely on examining geographic differences in voltinism instead of tracking long-term changes in voltinism in response to recent climate warming. For the purpose of this study, I collected data on a subset of North European Macrolepidoptera (altogether 731 species) with a wide latitudinal distribution range (15° latitudinal degrees as a minimum). Such species experience a wide range of season lengths which makes it unlikely that constraints imposed by season length alone could preclude multivoltinism throughout entire distribution ranges. Consistently, I interpret the occurrence of multivoltinism at any part of a species range as an indication of the potential for species to increase the number of generations with climate warming. By contrast, univoltinism throughout the distribution range will indicate the presence of some constraint – phylogenetic, ecological, genetic or physiological – precluding such response. My primary focus in this study is on revealing phylogenetic and ecological constraints to voltinism.

My analysis of moths and butterflies was facilitated by their remarkable diversity in body size and ecological traits, such as host use patterns (diet breadth, host type) and the timing of life cycle. Practical considerations for focusing on Macrolepidoptera were also important: for most moths and butterflies, at least in Europe, ecological data are readily available. Thanks to regular monitoring schemes and public interest, the variable to be explained, i.e. the number of generations per season is well documented for most species throughout their range.

## Material and methods

## Species selection

To examine the determinants of species’ capacity for multivoltinism, I compiled a list of Macrolepidoptera (Lasiocampoidea, Bombycoidea, Noctuoidea, Drepanoidea, Geometroidea, Papilionoidea) species with a wide latitudinal distribution in Europe. I started out with the list of Macrolepidoptera species that have been found in Finland (1040 species in total; as in Silvonen et al. 2014). For a species to be included, its distribution range in Europe had to exceed 60° latitude in the north (≈ southern border of Finland), and 45° latitude in the south.

Species not reaching 45° latitude in the south as well as those with clearly disjunct distribution ranges within Europe (which usually means that the southern populations are restricted to mountainous regions) were not considered (163 species in total). Additionally, I also excluded all migratory and occasionally occurring species (71 and 44 species, respectively). The latitudinal range (15 latitudinal degrees, about 1650 km) considered corresponds to a 6.5–8°C difference in mean annual temperatures (Helsinki, Finland 60°10′N 24°56′E: +5.9°C; selected European cities close to 45°: +12.3°C to +13.8°C). This latitudinal difference in mean annual temperatures is roughly comparable to longterm projections of climate warming (IPCC 2018). The actual latitudinal distribution ranges (and thus temperatures and day lengths experienced by different populations) of most species substantially exceed this minimum range required for species inclusion. Given the wide range of season lenghts over the latitudinal range considered, a species with a single generation per year throughout its whole European range was assumed to be obligately univoltine and retain a single generation per year with ongoing climate warming.

## Species data

For each species considered, ecological data were retrieved from the most comprehensive (series of) monographs on European Macrolepidoptera available, with the series ‘Noctuidae Europeae’ (vol. 1–13, 1990–2011) and ‘The Geometrid moths of Europe’ (vol. 1–6, 2001–2019) providing data for the highest number of species (see ‘Data sources’, for a full list of data sources; also some unpublished and expert data were used). The voltinism (= number of generations per year) of a species, the primary response variable in the analyses, was ascertained separately for the whole European distribution range of each species, and for its populations inhabiting latitudes higher than 60° (mainly based on Finnish data from Silvonen et al. 2014). In the analyses, all species were classified either as univoltine species or multi-voltine species. A species was considered univoltine if it has one generation per year throughout its European distribution range. A few species with a biennial (= semivoltine) life-cycle, i.e. species requiring more than one year for development, were also included in this category. By contrast, a species having two or more generations per year anywhere in its European distribution was considered multivoltine. Similarly, a species having a partial second generation anywhere in its range (i.e. some part of the population having a single generation whereas some part being bivoltine) was considered to have a potential for multivoltinism, and was thus classified as a multivoltine species. Note that there are very few species among the lepidopterans studied that have a multivoltine life cycle throughout their whole distribution range. At least the northernmost populations of the studied species are nearly always univoltine (or, in some cases, biennial).

As potential determinants of voltinism, I considered a set of traits that have previously been shown to be associated

with species’ phenologies (Altermatt 2010b, Diamond et al. 2011, Végvári  et  al. 2015): overwintering stage, larval diet breadth, host type and body size. 1) Based on overwintering stage, species were grouped as egg, larval, pupal or adult overwinterers. Close to 30 species reported to overwinter as fully formed adults within their pupal case (known also as pupal imagoes) were classified as adult overwinterers. This was done so because, unlike other (‘true’) pupal overwinterers and similar to adult overwinterers, pupal imagoes do not require extra time for pupal development in spring. In the overwhelming majority of Lepidoptera, overwintering stage is fixed and species-specific. However, two species, Pararge aegeria and Erebia ligea, reported to overwinter in different stages, were left unclassified. 2) Five categories were distinguished on the basis of host type used in the larval stage. A species was classified as a forb-feeder, if it feeds exclusively or mostly on non-graminoid herbs (= forbs) or/and low-growing prostrate shrubs (such as Vaccinium, Thymus or Calluna). A few polyphagous species feeding both on forbs and grasses were also classified as forb-feeders. However, a species strictly specialized to feed on graminoid herbs (= Poaceae, Cyperaceae and Juncaceae) was classified as a grass-feeder. Species feeding exclusively or mostly on woody plants (excluding low-growing prostrate shrubs) were classified as tree-feeders. A separate category was established for species feeding both on trees and herbs. Lichen-feeders were assigned a separate category, but due to their small number they could not be included in the main analyses. 3) Three categories were distinguished to quantify larval diet breadth: monophagous (species feeding on plants of a single genus), oligophagous (species feeding on several host plant genera of a single family, or on a few genera of a few families) or polyphagous (species feeding on many host genera of different host families; see Holm et al. 2018, for an identical approach). 4) Average wingspan (maximum distance from one forewing tip to the other forewing tip), calculated as an average of mean male and female wingspans, was used as the measure of body size. For a few species with short-winged females, this measure was based on male data only.

## Data analysis

Ecological correlates of the species’ potential for multivoltinism were evaluated using generalized linear mixed models with logit as the link function. Voltinism was used as a binary response variable (univoltine versus multivoltine) in the analyses. Larval diet breadth (mono- versus oligo- versus polyphagous), host plant type (woody plants, forbs, grasses, woody + herbaceous plants) and overwintering stage (egg, larva, pupa, adult) entered the full model as categorical explanatory variables, and body size as a continuous variable. Due to missing combinations of categorical variables in the data set, interactions could not be included in the model, but were subjected to detailed scrutiny using descriptive statistics. ## Results

## General patterns

Almost half (47.1%; 344 species) of the total of 731 widely distributed moth and butterfly species examined have just one generation per year (univoltine life-cycle) throughout their European range (Supplementary material Appendix 1 Table A1). In northern populations (Finland, above 60°N), about two thirds of all species considered are univoltine, confirming the general latitudinal decrease in voltinism to the north (discussed in Zeuss et al. 2017).

The probability of being invariably univoltine across latitudes appears to be associated with species traits, with certain trait values strongly lowering the species’ potential for multivoltinism. The model with the highest support (Akaike weight = 0.61, altogether 16 models) contained species size (relative importance = 1), overwintering stage (RI = 1) and host type (RI = 0.74) as predictors of voltinism. No other model received comparable support (i.e. $\Delta \mathrm { A I C _ { c } } \mathrm { \leq } 2 )$ Altogether, the fixed traits in the best model (i.e. species size, overwintering stage, host type) explained 44% of the variation in voltinism. The cross-model relative importance of larval diet breadth was low (RI = 0.21), and this variable did not appear in the best model.

## Overwintering stage

Among the ecological traits examined, overwintering stage has the strongest effect on the species’ potential for multi-voltinism (Table 1). The proportion of multivoltine species gradually increases from species overwintering as eggs to species overwintering as adults, larvae and pupae (Fig. 4). The respective patterns within three major superfamilies largely coincide with the general pattern (Supplementary material Appendix 1 Fig. A1). In species overwintering as eggs, multivoltinism is remarkably rare: just 8.8% of such species are fully or partly multivoltine at least in some part of their European distribution range. The proportion of multivoltine species among egg overwinterers is low irrespective of host type and larval diet breadth (Supplementary material Appendix 1 Table A2, A3).

The proportion of multivoltine species is also low in species overwintering as adults: about a quarter of these species (25.5%) have a partly or fully multivoltine life cycle anywhere in Europe (Fig. 4). The proportion of multivoltine species in the subset of adult overwinterers passing the winter as fully formed adults within their pupal case (pupal imagoes) was similar to the proportion of multivoltine species among the rest of adult overwinterers (28.6% and 22.2%, respectively). Univoltine species among adult overwinterers do not exceed one-third in any host type and larval diet breadth category, except for in the species-poor category of monophagous adult overwinterers (Supplementary material Appendix 1 Table A2, A3).

By contrast, multivoltine species comprise an overwhelming majority of pupal overwinterers: more than four fifths (81.0%) are partly or fully multivoltine within their European distribution range (Fig. 4). The proportion of multivoltine species among pupal overwinterers is uniformly high irrespective of host type and larval diet breadth (always ≥75%; Supplementary material Appendix 1 Table A2, A3). Species overwintering as larvae fall between these extremes, with

approximately half of such species (50.7%) being multivoltine (Fig. 4; Supplementary material Appendix 1 Table A2, A3).

## Host type

There is also a tendency, although marginally non-significant, for the species’ potential for multivoltinism to vary with host type (Table 1). The gap in the proportions of multivoltine species is widest between species feeding on graminoid plants (grasses, sedges and rushes), and those feeding on forbs and low-growing prostrate shrubs (Fig. 5). In the latter, 62.1% of the species are multivoltine, whereas in species feeding on graminoid plants just 28.2% of the species are multivoltine. Twenty-one species feeding internally within grass stems are univoltine with just one exception. Species feeding on woody plants or both on woody and herbaceous plants fall between these two figures, with multivoltinism occurring in 52.9% and 41.5% species, respectively (Fig. 5). Species feeding exclusively on coniferous trees have a somewhat higher incidence of multivoltinism (61.1% of 18 species) than other species feeding on woody plants. In lichen-feeding species, 40% of the 20 species have a multivoltine life cycle.

## Larval diet breadth

Larval diet breadth shows no overall association with voltinism (Table 1). In all three categories, i.e. mono-, oligo- and polyphages, multivoltine species comprise slightly more than half of all species (56.2, 54.2 and 51.2%, respectively).

## Discussion

The increase in the number of generations per year (i.e. voltinism) is one of the most commonly reported phenological responses to climate warming in insects (Altermatt 2010a, Pöyry  et  al. 2011). Nevertheless, the results of this study suggest that a considerable proportion of insect species are likely to maintain univoltine phenology despite increasing temperatures. The examination of 731 European moth and butterfly species with a wide latitudinal distribution (15° as a minimum) showed that only slightly more than half (52.9%) of these species are multivoltine in at least some part of their range. The rest of species have a single generation throughout their distribution range despite a high latitudinal variation in temperature and season length. My results show that a substantial proportion of the among-species variation in voltinism can be explained by species traits.

Focusing on the subset of (latitudinally) widely distributed species allowed me to evaluate their potential for multivoltinism in response to the levels of climate warming far beyond what we have experienced to date (+1°C increase; IPCC 2018), and comparable to long-term climate projections (IPCC 2018). The analysis indicates that the species’ potential for additional generations per year is strongly related to the factors that determine the overwintering stage of the species. The contrast is particularly strong between pupal overwinterers (81% multivoltine), on the one hand, and egg (9% multi-voltine) and adult (26% multivoltine) overwinterers, on the other hand. The life-histories of a considerable share of species in the two latter groups are typical of so-called spring- (or flush-) feeders (Hunter and Elkinton 2000, Van Asch and Visser 2007). Their eggs hatch when buds of woody plants begin to burst in the spring (adult overwinterers lay their eggs before bud burst), and the larvae are adapted to feed on bursting buds and young foliage of trees and shrubs, taking advantage of their high nitrogen content and low levels of defences. Because of declining nutritional quality and increasing defensive chemicals, maturing leaves become increasingly inedible for these species, with detrimental effects on larval growth and survival (Parry et al. 1998, Hunter and Elkinton 2000, Tikkanen and Lyytikainen-Saarenmaa 2002). Additional generations are therefore strongly disfavoured in spring-feeders on trees and shrubs.

Species feeding strictly on grasses form another major ecological group of lepidopterans with a low incidence of multivoltinism. Similar to most egg-overwintering spring-feeders on trees, all grass-feeders overwintering as eggs are univoltine. Nevertheless, multivoltinism is also infrequent among grassfeeders overwintering as larvae (28% multivoltine). The low incidence of multivoltine species among grass-feeders is plausibly related to the nutritional quality and seasonal dynamics of graminoid plants. Grasses typically contain primary nutrients at much lower concentrations than forbs and woody plants (Bernays and Barbehenn 1987, Tscharntke and Greiler 1995). Moreover, while in general phenologically young plants are nutritionally superior for herbivorous insects (Scriber and Slansky 1981), grasses are known to deteriorate in nutritional quality particularly rapidly (Bernays and Barbehenn 1987). The window of opportunity for strict grass-feeders may therefore be too short for additional generations to successfully develop, irrespective of the length of the season with favourable temperatures. The importance of host plant nutritional quality on voltinism has recently been demonstrated also by Pöyry et al. (2017), who showed that the number of generations per season increases with foliar nitrogen content of host plants. Endophagous grass-feeders seem to be particularly restricted in voltinism: none of the 21 species feeding internally within grass stems has more than one generation per year. Besides nutritional constraints, their window of opportunity is further restricted by the availability of sufficiently thick stems in which they can feed. On the other hand, the few grass-feeders overwintering as pupa (five

species) seem to have overcome host plant related constraints: all these species have multiple generations per year.

Larval diet breadth has no discernible overall effect on the species’ potential for multivoltinism. The proportion of multivoltine species among mono-, oligo- and polyphagous species is strikingly similar (51–56%). No doubt, in some cases, feeding on a single host plant species (or particular organs, such as fruits and flowers) that is available for a short period, eliminates the opportunity for multiple generations (e.g. Parnassius mnemosyne on Corydalis spp.: Välimäki and Itämies 2005; Eupithecia immundata on fruits of Actaea spicata: Ehrlén  et  al. 2015; Anthocharis cardamines on flowers and growing siliquae of Brassicaceae: Posledovich et al. 2015). However, the majority of strictly-specialized species utilize host species with less-restricted seasonal availability. Another plausible but to my knowledge untested explanation for multivoltinism in monophagous species is that being adapted to feed on a single host plant has allowed them to speed up their development, and pass multiple generations despite a shorter period of host availability and/or seasonally decreasing nutritional quality of the host plant. It should also be noted that classifying species by larval diet breadth across a large geographic area is not as straightforward as for local scales. On the one hand, several species that could have been classified as mono- or oligophagous at the local scale (e.g. for Finnish populations), were actually classified as oligo- or polyphagous species because of their much broader diet at the European level. On the other hand, a long list of potentially acceptable hosts in polyphagous species does not preclude a considerable preference for particular host species (Põldmaa et al. 2016).

The cross-species variation in the potential for increased voltinism with climate warming is likely to have considerable implications for population and community dynamics of Lepidoptera as well as associated lower and upper trophic levels (Altermatt 2010b). On the one side of the voltinism spectrum, the capacity for an increase in voltinism has been proposed to promote population growth, and increase the likelihood of outbreaks (Tobin et al. 2014; but see Hunter 1991). Recent studies have provided direct evidence that multivoltine lepidopterans in temperate and boreal environments are indeed generally more abundant (Mangels et al. 2017, Potocky et al. 2018), and show more positive population trends than do univoltine species (Pöyry et al. 2017, Macgregor et al. 2019; see also WallisDeVries and van Swaay 2017). On the opposite side of the spectrum, many univoltine species, particularly spring-feeders whose survival and development is strongly synchronized with their host phenology, are potentially vulnerable to phenological mismatch between interacting species that have been proposed to accompany climate warming (Visser and Holleman 2001, Forkner  et  al. 2008, Abarca and Lill 2015). Nevertheless, phenological advances have been most pronounced in egg- (Altermatt 2010b) and adultoverwintering Lepidoptera (Diamond et al. 2011), ecological categories in which the proportion of univoltine species is particularly high.
