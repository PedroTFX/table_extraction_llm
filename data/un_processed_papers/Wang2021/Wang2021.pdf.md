# Responses of ground beetle (Coleoptera: Carabidae) assemblages to stand characteristics and landscape structure in riparian poplar forests

BINLI WANG,<sup>1</sup> CHENGMING TIAN,<sup>1</sup> YINGMEI LIANG<sup>2</sup> and WEIHANG LIU<sup>1</sup> <sup>1</sup>TheKey Laboratory for Silviculture and Conservation of Ministry of Education, College of Forestry, Beijing Forestry University, Beijing, China and <sup>2</sup>Museum of Beijing Forestry University, Beijing Forestry University, Beijing, China

Abstract. 1. Many carabid beetles are among the most important biocontrol agents and are biological indicators of environmental change, although relatively little is known about how local and landscape factors affect forest ground beetle assemblages. Understanding such effects of multi-scale environmental drivers on ground beetles can promote carabid diversity and conservation.

2. Ground beetles were sampled by pitfall trapping in 40 poplar forest stands (sampling plots) along the Irtysh River in Northwestern China. We investigated responses of carabid activity density, species diversity and functional diversity (FD) to stand characteristics and surrounding landscape structure (the overall diversity and percentage cover of vegetation productivity classes in the surrounding area of each sampling plot based on normalised difference vegetation index).

3. While carabid activity density only responded positively to the overall diversity of vegetation productivity classes, carabid species diversity was positively associated with this landscape factor as well as vegetation cover but negatively associated with the nearest distance from sampling plot to the river. Only carabid FD responded significantly to percentage cover of vegetation productivity classes, showing a positive correlation with the proportion of medium vegetation productivity. Furthermore, overall carabid FD declined with increasing tree canopy cover.

4. Our results can help predicting spatial patterns of ground beetles according to landscape diversity and proportions of surrounding vegetation types and improve the design of conservation strategies (such as altering canopy cover) of carabids, especially forest specialist species. Any forest management strategies for promoting carabid diversity should consider the pervasive effects of landscape composition and configuration besides local stand characteristics.

Key words. Carabid beetles, functional diversity, landscape factor, riparian forests, species diversity, vegetation productivity.

## Introduction

Land-use intensification and land-cover change act as the main divers of biodiversity loss in forest landscapes while also leading to a decline in crucial ecosystem services (Straub et al., 2008; Lange et al., 2014). This is particularly true for riparian forest ecosystems, which can be found throughout much of the Northern Hemisphere (Philippe & Bohlmann, 2007; Araujo Calçada et al., 2015). For instance, vegetation cover in natural poplar forests along the Irtysh River in Northwest China are characterised by high spatial heterogeneity driven by enlarged farmland size and increasing grazing intensity (Lin et al., 2006; Wang et al., 2019). These processes are all potential causes for the loss of invertebrate biodiversity (Woodcock et al., 2003; Santos et al., 2019).

Conserving invertebrate biodiversity requires knowledge of effects of multiple environmental drivers at the local and landscape scales on arthropod groups, because most arthropod species experience their habitat at spatial scales beyond the plot level, especially natural enemies (Koivula & Vermeulen, 2005; Tscharntke et al., 2007; Vele et al., 2011; Lafage et al., 2015). Such multi-scale effects on natural enemies mainly depend on their dispersal ability, body size, and feeding habit (Jackson & Fahrig, 2012; Philpott et al., 2019). Generalist predators are known to strongly respond to factors at the landscape scale owing to high dispersal and colonisation capacity, while small and specialised natural enemies like egg parasitoids are always influenced by local factors (Chaplin-Kramer et al., 2011). Many ground beetles, which are generalist predators, have been widely recommended as bioindicators of environmental change at different spatial scales (Rainio & Niemelä, 2003; Woodcock et al., 2003, 2010; Alalouni et al., 2013; Borchard et al., 2014). Understanding the mechanisms that maintain fauna diversity and the associated services are key to improve effectiveness of conservation actions (Anton et al., 2010), especially given the current scenario of global changes (Dawson et al., 2011).

Carabid abundance, species diversity and functional diversity (FD) are likely to influence the provision of ecosystem services, and these broad characteristics can be used to assess the multiscale drivers of ground beetle assemblages (Woodcock et al., 2010; Spake et al., 2016; Philpott et al., 2019). Study on the effects of environmental factors on carabid biodiversity has traditionally utilised a taxonomic approach by focusing on their abundance or species richness (Batary et al., 2008; Fountain-Jones et al., 2015). But functional-trait analysis has several advantages over taxonomic approach in predicting various ecosystem functions and services (Kleyer et al., 2012; Fountain-Jones et al., 2015; Magura, 2017). FD captures the relative value, range or dispersion of functional traits in a community (Petchey & Gaston, 2002, 2006; Woodcock et al., 2014; Gallé et al., 2019). Thus, it is increasingly used as a metric to evaluate the responses of ground beetles to environmental change (Pavoine & Bonsall, 2011; Spake et al., 2016).

Ground beetles in meadow, urban or agricultural ecosystems have been proved to be influenced by both local and landscape factors (Woodcock et al., 2010; Lafage et al., 2015; Fusser et al., 2017; Philpott et al., 2019), and most studies of carabid diversity in forest ecosystems focused on effects of local forest management (Lange et al., 2014; Negro et al., 2014), forest stand types (Barsoum et al., 2014), or stand characteristics (Spake et al., 2016). However, relatively little is known about the combined and possible interactive effects of local stand factors and landscape structure of surrounding vegetation on forest ground beetle assemblages to our knowledge. As ground beetles have experienced general declines in diversity and abundance across natural forests in the northwest China, recognising the critical local and landscape-level factors affecting their abundance and diversity is important to develop comprehensive management strategies for promoting carabid diversity and conservation (Lafage et al., 2014, 2015; Rahman et al., 2015; Philpott et al., 2019).

In order to quantify the landscape structure of surrounding vegetation, landscape metrics is often derived from the classification of land-cover, which consists of a few discrete classes (Macfadyen et al., 2015). However, plant species in natural poplar forests could not fall neatly into certain habitat-type classes due to the high spatial heterogeneity of vegetation resources, where land cover is dominated by woodlands, grasslands, shrublands and farmlands (Vasseur et al., 2013). Gross primary productivity (GPP, hereafter vegetation productivity) is commonly used to characterise carbon-fixing ability of plants through photosynthesis in terrestrial ecosystems, which can mediate the relationship between land cover change and biodiversity (Running et al., 2004; Weiskittel et al., 2011; Alberti, 2015; LeVine & Crews, 2019). Therefore, in this study, landscape variables were generated from different vegetation productivity classes based on remotely sensed data (Lafage et al., 2014) in order to depict the spatial distribution of forest vegetation at the landscape scale and assess how this impacts carabid assemblages among different forest stands.

Here, we investigate how ground beetle activity density, species diversity and FD respond to stand characteristics (e.g. vegetation cover, tree canopy cover and the nearest distance from sampling plot to the river) and landscape structure of surrounding vegetation in 40 forest stands growing in natural poplar forests along the Irtysh River in Northwestern China. We used the overall diversity and percentage cover of vegetation productivity classes in buffers of 300 m radius surrounding all sampling plots as proxies for landscape structure of surrounding vegetation. Specifically, we hypothesised that (i) activity density of ground beetles would respond primarily to overall diversity of vegetation types in surrounding landscape, as many of them are highly mobile generalist predators and thus would benefit from different prey resources within forest landscapes; (ii) species diversity of ground beetles would also be higher in forest landscapes with higher diversity ofvegetation types based on the ‘habitat heterogeneity hypothesis’ (Tews et al., 2004); and (iii) ground beetle FD would show a greater dependence on landscape structure of surrounding vegetation than local stand factors, a response that is attributed to their high mobility needed for movement between dynamically variable habitat types (Woodcock et al., 2010).

## Ground beetle sampling

In each sampling plot, ground beetles were sampled by continuous pitfall trapping. As beetle catches in pitfall traps reflect the activity of the species, trap catches indicate ground beetle activity density, not necessarily abundance (Niemelä & Kotze, 2009; Philpott et al., 2019). On the diagonal of each sampling plot, five traps were installed 10 m apart on a transect that is parallel to the southern bank of river, and traps were almost level with each other. We inserted plastic cups (diameter = 6.5 cm, depth = 9.5 cm) into the ground by using a soil auger and ensured that the top surface of each pitfall trap flushed with the soil surface. We then used 120 ml of 50% ethylene glycol and water solution as a temporary preservative and a few drops of colourless, odourless detergent to reduce the surface tension (Gallé et al., 2019). Pitfall trapping was carried out over a 16-week period during the growing season from mid-May to mid-September for two consecutive years (2018 and 2019), because this period is generally associated with a maximum abundance of these arthropods. Pitfall traps were emptied fortnightly, for a total of eight times before the periodical mowing of the above-ground plants by local herdsmen. We transferred all samples into 70% ethanol for further identification. Adult ground beetles were identified to the species level by expert entomologists according to Berlov (2002), Dudko et al. (2010), and Kataev and Liang (2015) in the laboratory. All carabid specimens are stored at the Museum of Beijing Forestry University.

## Functional diversity

Three ecological traits that characterised functional diversity between ground beetle species included: body size (large vs. small), feeding trait (carnivorous vs. omnivorous), and wing morphology (macropterous vs. brachypterous) (Homburg et al., 2014) (Supporting Information Table S1). Body length was used as a measure of body size, which was measured as the distance from the tip of the mandibles to the end of the abdomen. Carabid species with an average body size equal to or longer than 15 mm were considered large; otherwise, they were considered small (Gobbi et al., 2007). Carabid species were considered carnivorous if they were strictly predators during their life cycle, whereas species that were occasional predators or herbivores were considered omnivorous (Gobbi & Fontaneto, 2008). For wing morphology, macropterous species were those with wings longer than the elytra, whereas brachypterous species were those with wings shorter than the elytra (Brambilla & Gobbi, 2014). Carabid FD was measured by calculating Rao’s quadratic entropy $\mathrm { ( F D _ { Q } ) }$ , which is a multi-trait FD metric that describes the diversity of a given community, combining the relative abundances of species with the biological information about the functional differences between species (Rao, 1982; Ricotta, 2005). $\mathrm { F D } _ { \mathrm { Q } }$ expresses the average functional dissimilarity between two randomly selected individuals if relative abundance is calculated from the number of individuals, and it has been widespread used to characterise human impacts on ecosystem processes (Aubin et al., 2013). We calculated $\mathrm { F D } _ { \mathrm { Q } }$ for each sampling plot as: $\mathrm { F D } _ { \mathrm { Q } } = \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { S } \sum _ { j = 1 } ^ { S } d _ { i j } p _ { i } p _ { j }$ where S is the number of species in a sampling plot, $d _ { i j }$ is the dissimilarity in trait values between each species pair i and $j ,$ and p is the proportion of individual number per species relative to the total individual number within a plot. Rao’s quadratic entropy was calculated by using the R package FD.

## Landscape structure ofsurrounding vegetation

Landscape structure of surrounding vegetation was measured in a 300 m radius buffer (Wang et al., 2019). The geographical coordinates of the centre of each sampling plot were collected using a handheld GPS unit. Then, based on these coordinates, open-access remote sensing images were acquired on 28 June 2018 for the whole study area covering six villages from the European Sentinel-2A’ satellite. We preprocessed remote sens ing images using ENVI version 5.4 software in order to eliminate errors and improve their quality, including radiation correction and atmospheric correction. The results of image-interpretation were rectified by adequate ground-truth data recorded in a forest vegetation survey. The normalised difference vegetation index (NDVI) is often used as a preferred surrogate for vegetation productivity of the terrestrial ecosystems (Voepel et al., 2011; Lezama et $a l .$ , 2014), particularly in applications involving land-cover change. Vegetation productivity was thus quantified by calculating NDVI values, which is defined as: $\mathrm { N D V I } = \mathrm { ( R _ { N I R } - R _ { r e d } ) } / ( \mathrm { R _ { N I R } + R _ { r e d } ) }$ , where R and $\mathrm { R } _ { \mathrm { r e d } }$ refer to the reflectance values derived from spectral radiances measured by the near-infrared channel and the red visible chan nel, respectively. NDVI values range from 1 (water) to 1 (maximum vegetation signal). We calculated NDVI values for each of 300 m radius buffers around sampling plots using ‘Band Math tool in ENVI version 5.4 software applied to the remote sensing image and classified NDVI values into five vegetation classes representing the intensity of the vegetation signal: (i) low vege tation ( 1.00 to 0.11); (ii) medium-low vegetation (0.11– 0.21); (iii medium vegetation (0.21–0.51); (iv) medium-high vegetation (0.51–0.81); and (vi) high vegetation signal (0.81– 1.00) (Fig. 1c). We distinguished each vegetation class based on a variation range examination of how NDVI values change in each habitat type (e.g. bare ground, shrubland, grassland, woodland, or farmland) across time. These vegetation productiv ity classes defined different habitat patches that had similar veg etation growth characteristics. for instance, NDVI values below 0.2 typically represent very low vegetation signal or bare ground Using five classes enabled us to separate the amount of each veg etation productivity class during the growing season precisely (Macfadyen et al., 2015). Finally, the proportion of each of the five vegetation productivity classes was calculated in a buffe of 300 m radius centred on each sampling plot. Shannon’s Diversity Index (SHDI) was also calculated by using Fragstats version 4.2 to quantify the overall diversity of vegetation pro ductivity classes.

## Stand characteristics

In each sampling plot, forest and vegetation structure was described by measuring a series of stand characteristics. Within the core area of each plot, diameter at 130 cm height [diameter at breast height (DBH)] and tree height of all living trees with $\mathrm { D B H } > 5 . 0$ cm were recorded. As DBH is often used as a proxy of tree height in most tree species, we only considered DBH as measure of tree size, which is an important structural trait, critical in forest ecology and for above ground biomass estimate. Canopy cover was assessed at four continuous 10 m 10 m assessment areas along the transect using a visual cover method (Humphrey et al., 1999). Percentage of vegetation cover on each plot was calculated from 10 random $1 \textrm { m } \times 1$ m quadrats along the transect (Rahman et al., 2015). We also measured species richness and average height of herbaceous plant species in three random $5 \mathrm { ~ m ~ } \times 5$ m sampling subplots within each sampling plot. An important environmental factor, the nearest distance from the centre of sampling plot to river bank (the distance to river, Magagula, 2003; Rykken et al., 2007) was measured by using ENVI version 5.4 based on satellite images.

## Statistical analysis

At each sampling plot, the activity density and species richness data of ground beetles from the five pitfall traps on each transect and for the two sampling years were pooled for statistical analyses. The responses of carabid species diversity and FD to landscape structure of surrounding vegetation and stand characteristics were assessed using linear mixed-effect models (LMMs) for each response variable. The ‘lmer' function (R package lme4; Bates et al., 2015) were used for species diversity of ground beetles, represented by the Shannon-Wiener’s diversity index (H0) (function ‘diversity’ in R package vegan; Oksanen et al., 2019) and FD of ground beetles $\mathrm { ( F D _ { Q } ) }$ as they are continuous responses. We tested the effects of stand level and landscape factors on carabid activity density using generalised linear mixed-effect models (GLMMs) with a negative binomial distribution (function ‘glmer,nb’ in R package lme4: Bates et al., 2015) since overdispersion was detected (overdispersion test via R package qcc; Scrucca, 2014). For variable selection, we conducted Spearman’s correlation test to check for correla tions among the proportions of five vegetation productivity classes, the SHDI and all stand characteristics. There was a strong correlation between the DBH and canopy cover, and the latter has previously been observed to affect ground beetle assemblages in forests (Lange et al., 2014; Negro et al., 2014; Spake et al., 2016), thus DBH was not considered to be an explanatory variable for further analyses. Additionally, as species richness and average height of herbaceous plant species, the proportions of low and medium-low vegetation productivity showed a strong correlation (correlation coefficients > 0.7 ) with other explanatory variables (Supporting Information Table S2), we excluded them from further analyses (Yang et al., 2019). Variance inflation factor (VIF) values were calculated to assess collinearity in the remaining seven explanatory variables and were found to be <2.0, indicating that covariation between explanatory variables was not a problem (Dormann et al., 2013; Schirmel et al., 2018). Therefore, explanatory variables in all full models were SHDI, the proportions of medium vegetation productivity (Medium VP), medium-high vegetation productivity (Mediumhigh VP), high vegetation productivity (High VP), canopy cover, vegetation cover, and the distance to river. All explanatory variables were assigned as fixed effects, and village ID was included as a random effect in order to account for the non-independence of sampling plots within the same village. Furthermore, explanatory variables in all models were scale-centred to facilitate the model’s convergence and two-way interactions between landscape and stand variables were tested only, as their three-way interactions could not be explored further owing to the relatively large number of explanatory variables (Zuur et al., 2009).

For model simplification, we used an information-theoretic approach to identify the best set of models from global GLMMs \LMMs via Akaike’s Information Criterion corrected for small sample size (AICc) (Grueber et al., 2011). All possible additive models were constructed using maximum likelihood methods in package MuMIn (Barton, 2018). The input variables were standardised using Gelman and Su’s (2018) approach (function ‘standardise’ in R package arm), which was essential for interpreting the parameter estimates after model averaging. We applied full model averaging across all plausible models with <2 ΔAICc of the best-fit model (i.e., the model with the lowest AICc value) (Grueber et al., 2011; Schirmel et al., 2018). We calculated marginal $\mathbb { R } ^ { 2 } ( \mathbb { R } _ { \mathrm { m } } ^ { 2 } )$ and conditional $ { \mathrm { R } } ^ { 2 } (  { \mathrm { R } } _ { \mathrm { c } } ^ { 2 } )$ to assess the proportion of variance explained by fixed effects and by both the fixed and random effects and thus estimate the goodness-of-fit of all plausible models following Nakagawa and Schielzeth (2013). We also conducted Moran’s I test for the residuals of all models to test for spatial autocorrelation (function ‘moran.test’ in R package spdep) (Bivand, 2018) and found no evidence of spatial autocorrelation. All statistical analyses were performed in R version 3.5.1 (R Core Development Team, 2018).

## Results

A total of 5503 ground beetles, belonging to 16 species, were collected during the two-year sampling period. The most abundant carabid species were Amara curta, Poecilus cupreus, Anchomenus dorsalis, Anisodactylus binotatus, Agonum impressum and Poecilus punctulatus in forest landscapes, representing 70.4% of the total carabid catches. The majority of collected ground beetles were small and medium-bodied predators (68.5% of individuals), and overall 74.5% of the total individuals were capable of flying (Supporting Information Table S1).

Effects ofenvironmentalfactors on activity density ofground beetles

Results of GLMMs and LMMs showed that ground beetle activity density, species diversity and FD responded differently to landscape structure and stand characteristics, but explanatory variables had no significant interaction effects (Supporting Infor mation Table S3).

Activity density of ground beetles varied among different poplar forest stands (36–817 individuals per plot over the sampling period. The results of multi-model inference showed that the model ‘SHDI + Dis. to river’ was the best ranked model (Supporting Information Table S3). The summarised results of the model averaging showed that landscape structure of surrounding vegetation had the strongest effect on carabid activity density, but stand characteristics had no significant effect on it (Table 1). More specifically, carabid activity density significantly increased with increasing the overall diversity of vegetation productivity classes (Estimate = 0.219, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2a). The value for the variance that is taken by the random terms was 0.017.

## Effects ofenvironmentalfactors on species diversity ofground beetles

Species diversity of ground beetles varied among different poplar forest stands (0.43–2.00 per plot over the sampling period). It responded strongly to both landscape structure of surrounding vegetation and stand characteristics (Table 1). The model ‘SHDI + Medium VP + High VP + Vegetation cover + Dis. to river’ was the best ranked model for species diversity of ground beetles, (Supporting Information Table S3). While the species diversity of ground beetles responded positively to the overall diversity of vegetation productivity classes (Estimate = 0.671, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2b), the proportion of each vegetation productivity class was not found to have a significant effect on carabid species diversity. Furthermore, carabid species diversity was positively associated with vegetation cover (Estimate = 0.286, P < 0.001) (Fig. 3a) but negatively associated with the nearest distance from sampling plot to the river (Estimate = 0.148, P < 0.001) (Fig. 3b). The value for the variance that is taken by the random terms was 0.045.

## Discussion

Our study revealed that landscape structure and stand characteristics had different effects on activity density, species diversity and FD of ground beetles in natural riparian poplar forests. In particular, all three broad characteristics of ground beetles correlated with landscape structure of surrounding vegetation, which suggested that spatial distribution of vegetation productivity is a good proxy for landscape structure explaining species composition and distribution patterns of ground beetle assemblages in forest ecosystems (LeVine & Crews, 2019). Although habitats in riparian border within natural poplar forests had different vegetation cover and soil moisture (Wang et al., 2019), the landscape classification map of riparian habitats by using traditional methods of classification could not result in a good explanation because it was difficult to classify mosaic-pattern vegetation into certain land-cover class neatly (Vasseur et al., 2013). Our findings are thus based on the classification of five vegetation productivity classes and these findings might help improve landscape-scale forest management strategies for conserving ground beetle diversity (Weiskittel et al., 2011; Lange et al., 2014; Macfadyen et al., 2015).

## Effects oflandscape diversity on carabid activity density

Significant positive effect of the overall diversity of vegetation productivity classes on carabid activity density did support our first hypothesis that activity density of ground beetles would respond primarily to landscape structure of surrounding vegetation. Vele et al. (2011) investigating the effects of local site conditions and landscape factors on ant and carabid community composition in a spruce forest, found that a greater quantity of suitable habitat in the surrounding landscape increased the probability of greater abundance of carabids in both optimal and suboptimal patches, similar to our result. Philpott et al. (2019) also demonstrated that landscape factors (gardens with higher crop richness and that are surrounded by agricultural land) influenced carabid activity density. Many ground beetles are highly mobile generalist predators, they can inhabit a variety of habitats that differ in their food resources, especially in terms of prey species and abundance (Woodcock et al., 2010). The great reliance on multiple prey resources over their lifetimes can increase the dependence of ground beetles on landscape structure of the surrounding vegetation (Purtauf et al., 2005; Fusser et al., 2017). As the diversity of habitats within a landscape increases, so too will variability in the abundance of potential prey items in each landscape habitat parcel (Rooney et al., 2008). Therefore, the overall diversity of different vegetation classes in the landscape can enhance their movement between prey resources within different habitats and thus increase the total activity density of ground beetles within a forest landscape, which was shown to be the case in this study.

## Effects oflandscape diversity and standfactors on carabid species diversity

In accordance with our second hypothesis, we found that carabid species diversity was positively correlated with overall diversity of vegetation productivity classes. The ‘habitat heterogeneity hypothesis’ is one of the most important basis of landscape ecology. Habitat heterogeneity is often associated with higher biodiversity in ecosystems (Benton et al., 2003). It is considered that habitats with higher spatial heterogeneity in vegetation contain more microclimatic conditions and ecological niches to meet survival needs of more arthropod species and thus increase their species diversity on multiple spatial scales (Davidowitz & Rosenzweig, 1998). Tews et al. (2004) reviewed studies on the relationship between habitat heterogeneity/ diversity and animal species diversity in a selected literature survey and found that majority of studies showed a positive correlation. Increasing landscape habitat diversity is generally associated with increase in species diversity of generalist predators (Gardiner et al., 2009; Chaplin-Kramer et al., 2011). This was also found to be the case for species diversity of ground beetles that showed a positive correlation with landscape habitat diversity. Many ground beetles show a preference for more than one potential habitat and their prey are not restricted to one particular habitat (Barsoum et al., 2014; Fusser et al., 2017). As habitat diversity increases, so too does the chance of encountering ground beetle species associated with these different habitat types at a particular sampling location. For mobile carabid species, heterogeneous landscapes with a greater range of resources and microclimates can generate more stable population dynamics (Oiver et al., 2010), so carabid species diversity was associated with higher habitat diversity of surrounding landscape (Taboada et al., 2006; Balzan et al., 2016).

Additionally, species diversity of ground beetles responded strongly to stand characteristics, supporting the results of a research aimed to examine the influence of natural areas on carabid communities, which found that environmental factors at both local and landscape scales influenced ground beetle diversity and community composition (Werling & Gratton, 2008). Vele et al. (2011) also indicated that carabid community composition was strongly affected by both local conditions near the pitfall traps and the availability of optimal habitat in the surrounding landscape. In this study, at the local scale, vegetation cover and the nearest distance from sam lin lot to the river were found to be the two most influential stand characteristics for carabid species diversity. Vegetation cover and distance from running/ standing water are known to be the main determinants of carabid beetle fauna (Magagula, 2003). Several previous studies suggested that species diversity of carabid beetles had a strong relationship with local habitat factors. and vegetation cover was found to be the most influential local factor for increasing carabid species diversity (Niemelä et al., 2002; Rahman et al., 2015). Vegetation cover can modify microclimatic conditions and provide ideal living space to promote the community establishment ofcarabid beetles and thus increase diversity of different carabid species (Rahman et al., 2015). Schlegel & Riesen (2012) also showed that high vegetation cover turned out to be the crucial habitat factor for carabid species composition and diversity. Furthermore, riparian border, a habitat with higher vegetation cover, was shown to have the highest species diversity of carabid species (Magagula. 2003) In riparian border, streams can change microclimate environment such as humidity and temperature of the adiacent terrestrial habitat. resulting in diverse animal communities (Lavallee & Richardson. 2010). A study on terrestrial ground beetle communities suggested that riparian habitats had a great influence in maintaining their species diversity in forests, in part because the changing microclimate in riparian border is the determining factor in habitat choices of different round beetles (R kken et al., 2007). Therefore, in natural poplar forest ecosystems, the higher vegetation cover can support more ground beetle species that rely on diverse microclimates ofriparian habitats. This study demonstrated the potential importance of vegetation cover and the distance to the river to carabid species diversity

Effects ofcanopy cover and landscape structure on carabid functional diversity

We found that local and landscape-level factors all strongly affected carabid FD, which is inconsistent with our third hypoth esis that FD of ground beetles would show a greater dependence on landscape structure of surrounding vegetation than local stand factors. However, this finding is in accordance with Gallé et al. (2019), who demonstrated that landscape configuration, management method and plot position drive carabid FD. Our results indicated that carabid FD decreased with increasing tree canopy cover, which was the most important stand factor at the local scale. For ground beetles, open-habitat species have different functional traits from forest specialist species especially in hunting strategies and dispersal abilities (Fuller et al., 2008; Jelaska & Durbešic, 2009). Lower percentages of canopy cover generally cause a lower humidity and a higher temperature but can also promote the growth of grasses and shrubs, which are important habitats for open-habitat carabids, providing ample space to colonise, disperse, and hunt (Negro et al., 2014). It means that the more open forest structure and the more diverse vegetation on the forest floor may promote immigration of addi tional open-habitat carabid species (Lange et al., 2014). There fore, ground beetles may easily re-establish as the forest canopy becomes less continuous caused by tree mortality or enlarged farmland size in this study. Our finding of decreasing carabid FD with increased canopy cover can be attributed to a deduction of open-habitat species in forest stands with higher canopy cover (Spake et al., 2016). In addition, the positive relationship between carabid FD and the proportion of medium veg etation productivity could be due to a fact that the medium-class vegetation productivity in this study was mainly associated with the percentage of grasslands in the surrounding landscape. At the landscape level, the proportion of open vegetation patches, such as non-forest vegetation areas, within 300 m of a trap appears to be an important factor for ground beetles (Vele et al., 2011). Grasslands in the landscape are important habitats for openhabitat species in forest ecosystems and support a wide variety of ground beetle species (Woodcock et al., 2005; Rahman et al., 2015). Successful migration of ground beetles is a key aspect for their survival, which indicates that the movement of open-habitat species can be promoted by the presence of grasslands nearby (Bodin & Norberg, 2007; Jelaska & Durbešić, 2009). Due to the limited dispersal ability of many forest specialist species, colonisation of non-forest vegetation areas by these species depends on landscape elements such as the proximity to source habitats and matrix quality (Koivula et al., 2002). In contrast to open-habitat species, forest species are reluctant to cross open habitat (Fuller et al., 2008). As FD of ground beetles strongly responded to landscape structure of surround vegetation, the great complementarity in functional traits associated with different hunting strategies and dispersal ability would be likely to sustain functionally diverse ground beetle communities and potential biocontrol at a large spatial scale (Straub et al., 2008; Woodcock et al., 2010). FD indices have been used more often in evaluation of habitat change effects because they are more sensitive to local habitat quality and landscape structure and are more relevant to ecosystem services than species diversity indices (Spake et al., 2016; Baulechner et al., 2019).

## Conclusions

Our results highlight that overall carabid activity density, species diversity and FD respond differently to both local stand characteristics and landscape structure of surrounding vegetation, so large-scale forest management for conserving carabid diversity must focus on whole assemblages. Significantly, we found that carabid FD, a potentially important factor in evaluating the impact of forest management strategist, can be promoted by relatively simple manipulation for proportions of surrounding vegetation types, since this can be achieved easily and costeffectively by increasing clusters of grasslands. The weak response of carabid FD to vegetation diversity suggests that any change in landscape composition may be insufficient to result in a significant increase in carabid FD. Our investigation of the drivers of carabid FD amongst natural riparian forest stands in Northwest China has shown that canopy cover is an important stand characteristics of carabid FD, with increasing canopy cover tending to reduce FD. This suggests that management efforts that promote carabid FD should make canopy cover less continuous by creating canopy gaps. Therefore, any management intervention for maximising diversity of ground beetles in forests should consider the pervasive effects of landscape composition and configuration besides local-scale stand factors.
