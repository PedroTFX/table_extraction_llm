# Life-history trait and functional diversity patterns of ground beetles and spiders along a coastal heathland successional gradient

Jens Schirmel<sup>a,b,</sup>∗, Irmgard Blindow<sup>a</sup>, Sascha Buchholz<sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Biological Station of Hiddensee, University of Greifswald, Biologenweg 15, 18565 Isle of Hiddensee, Germany <sup>b</sup>Institute for Environmental Sciences, University of Koblenz-Landau, Fortstr. 7, 76829 Landau, Germany <sup>c</sup>Department ofEcology, TU Berlin, Rothenburgstr. 12, 12165 Berlin, Germany

Received 12 April 2012; accepted 28 August 2012

## Abstract

We investigated patterns in species richness and diversity, life-history traits and functional diversity of carabid beetles and spiders along a coastal heathland successional gradient. We sampled in five successional stages on the island of Hiddensee, Germany. Species richness of carabids and spiders and Simpson diversity for spiders did not differ among stages. Functional dispersion (FDis), a measure of functional richness, was lowest in the youngest stage for both carabids and spiders. Older successional stages represent more heterogeneous habitats and offer a broader range of niches, which might enhance functional dispersion. Functional evenness (FEve) differed among successional stages for spiders, indicating an uneven distribution of species abundances in the functional trait space. Functional divergence (FDiv) did not differ among stages for either taxon. Shifts in life-history traits were noted in both arthropod groups, but not always in the same direction: Body size of carabid beetles increased towards older successional stages, but decreased for spiders. The number of flightless carabid species increased towards older habitats. Each successional stage seemingly favors species with distinct life-history traits (species sorting). We conclude that a trait-based approach including the use of functional diversity measurements (FDis) could contribute valuable information for biodiversity conservation.

## Introduction

Succession in open terrestrial ecosystems is a key driver of biodiversity dynamics and can have differential effects on invertebrate diversity (e.g., Balmer & Erhardt 2000; Marini, Fontana, Battisti, & Gaston 2009; Buchholz 2010). In heathlands, without management the depletion of nutri ents is inhibited and vegetation undergoes succession towards shrub or tree dominated vegetation (Webb, 1998). In such ecosystems, successional processes often are detrimental to open-habitat specialist or xerothermic species due to e.g., loss of disturbance and dynamic effects (Bonte, Lens, & Maelfait 2006; Schirmel & Buchholz 2011). For analyzing shifts in terrestrial ecosystem conditions the ground-dwelling arthropods carabid beetles (Coleoptera: Carabidae) and spiders (Araneae) are suitable indicator taxa (carabids: e.g., Lövei, Magura, Tothmeresz, & Ködöböcz 2006; Lin, James, & Dolman 2007; spiders: e.g., Huber, Schulze, & Baumgarten 2007; Horvath, Magura, Szinetar, & Tothmeresz 2009).

Within the framework of biodiversity and conservation studies, species richness is usually the simplest and most intuitive measure for diversity (Magurran 2004). Several diversity indices have been developed to incorporate the relative abundance of species (or the evenness): of these the Simpson index D is intuitively meaningful and provides good diversity estimates (Magurran 2004). Both species richness and (Simpson) diversity have often been used in ecological studies to describe assemblage shifts and they have proven useful tools to assess and monitor changes caused by environmental variability (e.g., Blaum, Seymour, Rossmanith, Schwager, & Jeltsch 2009; Negro, Isaia, Palestrini, Schoenhofer, & Rolando 2010; Schirmel, Timler, & Buchholz, 2011). Apart from these ‘traditional’ diversity measures, the concept of ‘functional diversity’, which integrates information on life-history traits has grown rapidly in recent years in ecological research (Petchey & Gaston 2006; Violle et al. 2007; Il Choi et al. 2010), since the realization that life-history traits also have an important role to play in diversity (Ribera, Dolédec, Downie, & Foster 2001; Da Silva, Aguiar, Niemelä, Sousa, & Serrano 2007; Vandewalle et al., 2010). Zoological studies have mainly applied the trait concept to freshwater and marine environments (Bremner, Rogers, & Frid 2006; Diaz, Alonso, & Gutierrez 2008), but also to terrestrial arthropods (Keil, Dziock, & Storch 2008; Lambeets, Vandengehuchte, Maelfait, & Bonte 2008; Lambeets, Vandegehuchte, Maelfait, & Bonte 2009). Functional diversity concepts can provide a useful approach to integrate biodiversity research into the broader context of ecosystem processes and functioning (Loreau 2010), e.g., for productivity (Tilman et al. 1997) or resilience (Folke et al. 2004). Several indices were developed analogously to traditional alpha diversity indices to measure functional diversity or to display functional composition (Petchey & Gaston 2006; Villéger, Mason, & Mouillot 2008; Laliberté & Legendre 2010). In the present study, three indices were used to explore the different facets of functional diversity: functional evenness (FEve) and functional divergence (FDiv) (Villéger et al. 2008) as well as functional dispersion (FDis) (Laliberté & Legendre 2010). Although it has an extensive theoretical and mathematical background (Schleuter, Daufresne, Massol, & Argillier 2010), functional diversity, especially from multiple traits, has rarely been incorporated into ecological studies to date, and little is known in particular about shifts of functional diversity along environmental gradients (Gerisch et al. 2011). However, life-history traits of carabids and spiders have already been successfully integrated into investigations of assemblage shifts (e.g., Lambeets et al. 2008; Lambeets et al. 2009; Il Choi et al. 2010; Vandewalle et al. 2010).

In their metacommunity theory, Leibold et al. (2004) argue that the community composition in spatially structured, heterogeneous environments – such as coastal heathlands – could be altered by ‘species sorting’. In species sorting, resource gradients cause differences in species composition because each species becomes a specialist for different local habitat conditions. The concept presumes that local habitat templates can act as filters sorting species according to their functional traits (Leibold et al. 2004). As a consequence, only species with functional traits that match the specific environment can pass these filters (Poff 1997).

In particular, we (i) tested whether species richness and diversity as well as functional diversity increased with age and structurally complexity of successional stage and (ii) hypothesized that succession is reflected by shifts in lifehistory traits, and (iii) that certain traits are responsible for species sorting along a successional gradient.

## Sampling of carabid beetles and spiders

A total of 15 plots (three replicates per successional stage) were established. Each plot was $5 0 0 \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 }$ and had a homogeneous vegetation structure typical for the stage in question. Carabid beetles and spiders were sampled using pitfall traps. Pitfall trapping is a standardized method widely used for sampling ground-dwelling arthropods, producing high catch numbers and useful data on activity densities (New 1998). In each plot, four pitfall traps were randomly set level with the soil surface, but at a distance of at least 5 m from each other to minimize potential interference and to the boundaries to minimize edge effects. The traps were 6 cm in diameter, 8 cm deep and filled with a 70% ethylene-glycol solution and a few drops of detergent. A 15 cm 15 cm transparent plastic roof was placed a few cm above the trap to exclude litter infall and precipitation. Pitfalls were active between 15 May 2008 and 04 May 2009 and were emptied monthly. We sampled year-round to avoid possible biases due to interspecific differences in seasonal activities. Thus, our dataset delivered information on the activity densities of individual species in a specific habitat (e.g., Baars 1979; Ribera et al. 2001).

Catches were sorted into taxa and stored in 70% alcohol. Carabid beetles were identified to species level according to Müller-Motzfeld (2006), spiders according to Roberts (1987, 1998) and Nentwig et al. (2003). Only adult spiders were included in the analysis. Nomenclature follows

## Life-history traits

Carabid beetles and spiders were assigned to lifehistory trait categories with the help of literature data and using morpho-physiological and phenological features which potentially affect the occurrence patterns of these species (Ribera et al. 2001; Bonte et al. 2006; Lambeets et al. 2009) (Table 1; Appendix A: Table 1). The following traits were analyzed: ‘body size’, ‘dispersal’ and ‘feeding preference (carabids)/hunting mode (spiders)’. For body size, metric data (mm) were given in addition to body size categories.

## Data analysis

## Diversity

For each plot, data from the four pitfall traps were pooled, and species and individual catch numbers were used for statistical analyses. Species richness was expressed as number of species per plot and we chose the Simpson index as a diversity measure (1-D).

We used three functional diversity measurements. FDis expresses changes in functional dispersion, FEve for functional evenness and FDiv for functional divergence. FDis is a measure of functional richness, which considers species relative abundances by estimating their dispersion in a multidimensional trait space (Laliberté & Legendre 2010). In the following, we interpret functional dispersion as a measure for functional diversity per se. Life-history trait data (for dispersal, feeding preference/hunting mode) were coded categorically for calculating functional diversity indices except body size. All used indices are distance-based and can be computed for categorical variables (Laliberté & Legendre 2010; Laliberté & Shipley 2010; Schleuter et al. 2010). We computed FDis (Laliberté & Legendre 2010) and FEve and FDiv (Villéger et al. 2008) using the R language package FD (Laliberté & Shipley 2010). Differences in species richness, Simpson diversity and functional diversity indices among the successional stages were tested with generalised linear models (GLMs) using Poisson models for count data and Gaussian error structures and identity link function for indices.

## Life-history traits

To detect possible differences in life-history trait composition among successional stages, we applied GLMs with the number of species of a trait category per plot as the dependent variable (e.g., number of very small spider species per plot). To compensate for overdispersion, the standard errors were corrected using a quasi-Poisson model (Zuur, Ieno, Walker, Saveliev, & Smith 2009). In addition, mean body size of carabid beetles and spiders were compared among the five successional stages using one-way ANOVA.

The free software package R 2.11.1 (R Development Core Team 2010) was used for all analyses.

## Life-history traits

For the tested life-history traits, frequencies of trait categories within plots differed significantly among the five stages (Fig. 3). Carabid beetle body size categories differed significantly and mean number of very small species was highest in grey dunes while mean number of large species was highest in the two oldest stages. Accordingly, mean body size differed significantly among stages and was lowest in grey dunes. Dispersal ability differed significantly among stages, with the lowest number of flightless species occurring in grey dunes. Feeding preferences of carabid beetles differed in two categories: Collembola predators were most species rich in the oldest stages heath with shrubs and birch forest, while the highest number of phytophagous species occurred in grey dunes.

In contrast to carabid beetles, most large and medium-sized spider species were found in grey dunes and the mean body size also decreased from young to older habitats. Spiders with no ballooning ability were most species rich in grey dunes, followed by dwarf-shrub heath. Species richness of actively hunting spiders was highest in grey dunes.

## Discussion

## Diversity

With respect to the alpha-diversity measurements species richness and Simpson diversity, differences among the five successional stages were only found in Simpson diversity for carabid beetles (lowest in grey dunes). For spiders, no changes in species richness and Simpson diversity were detected among successional stages, which is in accordance with the findings of previous studies (e.g., Buddle, Spence, & Langor 2000; Gajdos & Toft 2000; Mallis & Hurd 2005). In contrast, Cristofoli, Mahy, Kekenbosch, and Lambeets (2010) showed that spider diversity increased towards older successional stages of wet heathlands. However, diversity is a matter of scale, and mechanisms such as species sorting might be responsible for structuring carabid beetle and spider assemblages or causing assemblage shifts (Lambeets et al. 2009). In species sorting, species distribution is linked to local conditions (niche separation) and is independent of unrelated purely spatial effects (Leibold et al. 2004). Community composition might therefore change at each successional stage by replacing one specialist by another (including shifts in lifehistory traits) without affecting the overall diversity of the community at the given stage (Buchholz 2010).

For both taxa, FDis was significantly lowest at the grey dune stage. Low functional diversity might be related to a lower number of available niches. Moreover, it might indicate a reduced productivity, since some resources potentially available to the community are unused or not available (Petchey, Evans, Fishburn, & Gaston 2007; Villéger et al. 2008). However, the dominance of a few specialized species, which have become accustomed to (or have adapted to) disturbance effects and extreme microclimate conditions in these young, open, and dynamic habitats might also be responsible for lower functional diversity. In contrast, the older successional stages represent more heterogeneous and complex habitats than the grey dunes and therefore offer broader niche ranges. This could enhance functional diversity and might lead to more (functional) complex species assemblages. High values of FEve indicate low habitat dynamics and balanced niche occupancy (Schleuter et al. 2010). Villéger et al. (2008) noted that both functional and species evenness are closely related. Thus, FEve should be highest in older successional stages with reduced habitat dynamics and lowest in dynamic grey dunes, which corresponded to our results for spiders in this study.

Most obviously, habitat heterogeneity facilitates niche differentiation in coastal heathlands (Schultz & Finch 1996); this is reflected by a higher functional diversity (FDis) ofcarabid beetles and spiders in later and structurally more complex successional stages. Our findings therefore emphasize that species richness (and to some extent Simpson diversity) have limited explanatory power for analyzing shifts along environmental gradients, as also found e.g., by Brose (2003), Mallis and Hurd (2005) and Buchholz (2010).

## Life-history traits

Changes in functional diversity (FDis) were linked to community-wide character shifts of both carabid beetles and spiders. Habitat conditions constrained species composition (Schirmel & Buchholz 2011) but varied among functional groups. Species sorting occurred, allowing species with distinct life-history traits to occur under the given conditions of a certain successional stage.

Carabid beetles were smaller in grey dunes and large-bodied in most vegetated successional stages. This corroborates the stress hypothesis according to which species often have a smaller body size in disturbed and dynamic habitats (Gray 1989). As an adaption to unstable conditions, species in more disturbed habitats often have a high turnover rate and therefore a smaller body size, while species in undisturbed habitats often have a low turnover rate and a larger size. For carabid beetles, support for this hypothesis had already been given by Blake, Foster, Eyre, and Luff (1994), Ribera et al. (2001) and Niemelä and Kotze (2009) who found that body size was smaller in disturbed habitats.

Opposite patterns were observed in spiders, where most large and medium-sized species occurred in grey dunes, implying that body size patterns seem to be constrained by morphology rather than habitat dynamics. Remmert (1981) and Entling, Schmidt-Entling, Bacher, Brandl, and Nentwig (2010) suggested that dry and hot habitat conditions favor large-bodied species because of the higher desiccation resistance which results from their more compact and waterproof cuticle and their lower surface area: volume ratio. Moreover, large spider species with a burrowing life style often occur in dune habitats which benefit from high sand dynamics that avoid an increase in soil hardness and vegetation encroachment (Bonte et al. 2006). However, as only body size values of adults were used for analyses it is possible that patterns may change substantially after including all instars.

In temporary habitats, many carabid species are highly mobile and able to fly as an adaption to fluctuations in habitat availability (Greenslade & Southwood 1962; Bonte, Baert, & Maelfait 2002). Consequently, the numbers of species with flightless ability were highest in the older successional and more stable stages. Spider species are able to disperse by ballooning (Bell, Bohan, Shaw, & Weyman 2005), and as far as carabids are concerned, it can be expected that the inhabitants of dynamic grey dunes are mostly good and early colonizers (Bonte et al. 2002, 2006; Buchholz 2010). We found a contrasting pattern that might be related to larger body sizes in these habitats, since ballooning ability is associated with small species and juvenile stages (Bell et al. 2005). However it should be noted that the ballooning ability estimates were derived from the literature. This may have reduced the accuracy of our estimates, since ballooning propensity may vary among different populations and because literature data may refer to different communities and ecosystems. In (coastal) heathland ecosystems – often consisting of a small-scale mosaic of different vegetation types – running can be a more adequate dispersal strategy as it enables fast and directed migration between grey dunes and nearby heathland or grass habitats, for example during unfavorable time periods (Bonte, Maelfait, & Hoffmann 2000; Bonte et al. 2002). Mass effects are also possible, as densely vegetated successional stages may constitute a source for grey dune spider assemblages, especially for lycosid species (Öberg, Ekbom, & Bommarco 2007).

Most carabids specialized in feeding on Collembola occurred in older successional stages, these being shady and more humid habitats that presumably favor higher Collembolan abundances. Active hunting spiders were dominant in open grey dunes, as previously shown by Finch (1997). Due to lower spatial resistance active hunting species, such as Lycosidae, are favored, while ambush and web hunters require well-developed habitat architecture, which is usually not given in grey dunes.

## Conclusions

We present one of the first empirical studies to measure different components of functional diversity of ground-dwelling arthropods in response to coastal heathland succession. By means of the newly developed functional dispersion index (Laliberté & Legendre 2010) we showed that functional diversity of carabids and spiders differed along a successional gradient, while alpha-diversity remained unaffected. Functional diversity therefore appears to provide a useful tool to analyze various ways arthropods respond to environmental variability. Based on life-history trait shifts along the successional gradient we assume that trait-based environmental filtering plays a central role due to species sorting. This means that species sorting in which distinct life-history traits are favored at different successional stages is prevalent. Using the trait-based approach, including functional diversity indices such as FDis, could contribute valuable information for conservation. It is most important to maintain a mosaic of different successional stages, as functional diversity is enhanced at a landscape level and distinct life-history traits are promoted. Furthermore, conservation must focus on dynamic grey dunes, as these habitats filter (mostly rare and endangered) species adapted to extreme local conditions such as sand dynamics and hot microclimate.
